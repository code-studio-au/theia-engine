# THEIA — THE INTERWOVEN CHRONICLE

Before the world there was no first hour to measure. There was only a conflict between two primordial wills who were not yet gods in any sense mortals could have understood. **Aethyr** was the potential for things to become; **Nhal** the denial of that potential, the principle that something could be unmade. The long struggle was not a war fought across stars, since stars had not yet been born. It was a battle over whether there could be a world at all.

Aethyr could not win through force. Nhal could not wholly extinguish possibility. Yet Aethyr understood that continuing the old struggle would eventually permit Nhal to prevail. There remained only a terrible alternative. Aethyr could change the conditions of the conflict by ceasing to be the single consciousness that fought it.

It deliberately shattered itself.

The **First Sundering** released the creative force from which time, matter, space and fundamental magic emerged. Seven independent conscious inheritors awoke from the shards of Aethyr. Nhal was blasted beyond the newborn physical realm into the **Void**, which contained no ordinary space, life, matter, time or magical ambience. Nhal persisted because its existence was primordial, but the new laws of physical reality excluded it. This was not a conventional prison with bars and a gate. The separation later nicknamed **the Veil** was a property of existence itself. Magic would not be a gift granted by the Seven: it was the foundation of the realm in which they awoke.

The Seven did not know why they had been born. The fragments recognized something familiar in one another, but none retained the entire memory of Aethyr or the primordial war. No one was their king. They discovered their powers gradually, learned magical laws rather than inventing them, and developed independent personalities, ambitions and loyalties.

**Guardian** could protect and restore but was too ready to sacrifice, even what was not theirs to sacrifice. **Seeker** was driven to understand and sometimes mistook intellectual confidence for wisdom. **Warrior** could empower will and endurance but could not easily compromise honour. **Shaper** delighted in constructing and transforming creation and possessed an ambition that sometimes outran care. **Judge** understood binding, equilibrium and truth but might apply law inflexibly. **Keeper** could contain and stabilize extraordinarily dangerous powers but withdrew into secrecy. **Ascendant** understood amplification, adaptation and synthesis but envied the accomplishments for which Shaper received praise.

These were distinct talents, not mutually exclusive monopolies over spells. Some divine acts demanded that several gods combine knowledge and power; their independence made such cooperation a matter of trust.

Trust initially flourished. Guardian and Warrior became the closest friends, agreeing on the duty to protect but differing over whether honour could ever be set aside to save lives. Seeker and Shaper became brilliant collaborators, one discovering possibilities and the other making them tangible. Judge mediated a council of equals. Guardian worried about Keeper's isolation; Seeker challenged Keeper's guarded knowledge and Judge respected caution without accepting secret exceptions to common law. Warrior respected Judge and kept a distance from Ascendant, while Shaper's habit of dismissing Ascendant's refinements turned recognition into an old wound. Ascendant had no close divine ally. The seeds of tragedy were not wickedness but competing virtues pushed beyond their limits.

The first great tension among the Seven was not war but the question of what a creator owed to a creation. Guardian wanted living beings who could flourish independently, and was willing to protect them at extraordinary cost. Judge held that autonomy also required laws, obligations and protections from arbitrary divine will. Warrior believed a people should learn courage rather than dependence on a god's strength. Seeker delighted in the prospect of mortal minds that could contradict divine explanations. Shaper imagined cities, tools and lifeforms beyond anything the Seven had made. Keeper feared that a creation made without sufficient safeguards might one day destroy itself or its neighbours. Ascendant insisted that every being should be capable of adaptation and improvement beyond its maker's original design. None could yet foresee how those principles would later make cooperation almost impossible.

The gods' differences grew through relationships, not through seven isolated philosophical manifestos. Guardian and Warrior were close enough to disagree without immediately becoming enemies; Warrior's unyielding honour challenged Guardian's readiness to sacrifice. Seeker and Shaper built a genuine partnership, yet the Seeker's insistence on testing every claim could conflict with Shaper's attachment to a beloved creation. Judge's respect for Keeper did not include approval of unilateral secrecy. Keeper's distance from Guardian was emotional, not evidence of disloyalty. Shaper was especially beloved by ancient mortals, and Ascendant felt increasingly invisible in that admiration. These bonds would be the fault lines through which Nhal eventually worked.

The gods created peoples by shaping fundamental magic and divine essence into independent mortal life. **Guardian created elves** with deep ties to living systems. **Warrior created orcs**, whose martial potential was joined to sophisticated honour and independent culture. **Shaper created dwarves**, gifted builders, engineers and makers. **Judge created humans**, adaptive peoples capable of complex social institutions. **Keeper created halflings**, with a subtle ancestral affinity for magical resilience and stability. **Keeper and Ascendant together created trolls**, blending resilience and the potential for adaptation.

Keeper and Ascendant had once seen reason to collaborate. Keeper knew that a rigid pattern could fail when the world changed; Ascendant understood that unlimited amplification could tear apart the pattern sustaining it. A people combining resilience with adaptation seemed to require both philosophies. Keeper therefore approached the project as a shared responsibility rather than a concession to a rival.

The violation was discovered in the working account when Keeper examined variations in the new trolls' magical structure and found changes that Ascendant had not disclosed. The choice confronting Keeper was terrible in miniature: undoing the change could harm living people who had done nothing wrong, while accepting it might teach Ascendant that secret alterations were permissible. Keeper stabilized the people and chose not to punish them for their maker's actions. Ascendant saw the resulting suspicion as proof that innovation would always be treated as a crime. Keeper saw a partner who had made informed consent impossible. This was not the cause of the troll exile, which came much later through mortal religious conflict, but it became a divine precedent that coloured every subsequent argument over forbidden creation.

The trolls' making revealed a problem that would matter far beyond them. Keeper provided stable patterns; Ascendant thought those patterns could be elevated and secretly introduced a forbidden alteration without agreement. Once Keeper saw the change, removing it would risk harming beings already living. Keeper completed the stabilizing work instead. Trolls were not flawed or corrupted; the betrayal belonged to their creators, not to them. Yet the breach gave Keeper cause for future suspicion, Judge a precedent for lawful objections, Warrior a question of honour and Ascendant a grievance over unrecognized innovation. Centuries later Nhal would exploit precisely this history.

Mortals were never confined to their creators' intentions. The orcs developed scholars as well as champions; dwarves developed healers as well as masons; elves could choose life without wielding magic. Racial bloodlines inherited divine essence, ordinarily subtle and occasionally concentrated through rare empowered ancestral lineages. Creator and chief patron were not synonyms. Humans created by Judge would eventually principally worship Guardian; halflings created by Keeper would turn toward Seeker. This pattern of changed allegiance later became central to the civilizational divisions.

The six peoples spread into their ancestral habitats. Human settlements occupied great inland plains and river valleys where agriculture could support population and law. Dwarven mountain holds dug immense subterranean works, protected mines and built stable roads and forges. Orcish Great Clans arose across grasslands, establishing religious centres, martial traditions and permanent cities. Halflings settled underground valleys, building intricate guild-based systems, waterways and mechanisms. Elves cultivated primeval forests and systems of living and magical stewardship. Trolls inhabited rugged highlands, where stone, defensive wards and chronicles preserved continuity. These places did not become six immutable racial theme parks: migration, geography and trade gradually established varied local cultures.

The gods were distant from daily mortal life. Their intervention was rare and significant. Much more commonly, mortal ingenuity solved mortal problems. Shaper was widely loved because cities and irrigation systems were visible achievements; Seeker found mortal discoveries exciting; Guardian protected peoples other than elves; Judge watched human institutions grow beyond its founding intentions. Ascendant noticed how rarely its improvements were credited, deepening envy without making the god naturally evil.

Scholarship became a civilization of its own. Mortals learned to work with **exactly six magical disciplines**: **Elements & Energy**, **Matter**, **Life**, **Form**, **Mind** and **Passage**. All races could study each; aptitude and ancestry affected affinity, not absolute permission. Magic drew on finite internal reserves guided by intent, skill and learned precision, recovered naturally and through ambient energy or disciplined meditation. Simple work might use one discipline; complicated effects several, which one practitioner had to understand personally. Several untrained mortals could not fuse into a master spellcaster. Extremely ambitious workings could overload concentration and go wrong; exhaustion normally prevented further casting. Stable matter created from nothing, self-sustaining enchantments, extraordinary resurrection and far-ranging passages became rare ancient arts, not simple conveniences.

The great magical orders translated exceptional knowledge into ordinary civic dependence. Their libraries preserved failed experiments as well as successes; their scholars crossed political boundaries, challenged gods openly and taught in independent institutions. Founding divine teachings, occasional direct instruction and trusted mortal intermediaries all played roles. An engineer might use a halfling regulator to maintain a dwarven defensive structure; an elven Life specialist could debate a human healer over the conditions of recovery; Passage experts could connect distant markets and archives. The great orders were three or four in number, independent yet interconnected, mostly cooperative until the war. Their exact names were never ratified.

The abundance of magical infrastructure had a hidden cost. A city that relied on ancient passages for grain could become vulnerable when the road beside it fell into disuse. A settlement protected by a self-sustaining ward might forget how to defend itself without that ward. A magical work maintained by an expert lineage could become irreparable when that lineage died. These dependencies help explain why the later catastrophe produced starvation, migrations and centuries of loss rather than a single dramatic night after which life simply resumed.

The Age of Wonders was therefore not an age of unlimited sorcery. It was an age of extraordinary accumulated knowledge. A single skilled engineer could maintain a city ward constructed by predecessors; a series of separate expert-created structures could sustain a long-range passage; a forged item could be charged and renewed. Practical magic and conventional craft supported one another even when their schools competed for recognition.

**Three or four great independent magical orders** emerged beside many smaller schools. Their identities came from philosophy, specialties and public missions, not a one-to-one correspondence with the six disciplines. They maintained shared libraries, visited one another, held scholarly councils and used the ancient passages. They received divine founding instruction, rare appearances and trusted intermediaries, yet could openly challenge divine ideas. They argued over ethical research, ownership of discoveries and political influence, but were mostly cooperative until war destroyed that arrangement. No final names or exact number beyond “three or four” have been approved. Exactly one original great order would secretly survive the catastrophe; which one remains undecided.

This intellectual world made possible the most extraordinary and dangerous project the Seven undertook. A network of scholars wondered why certain kinds of magic could be combined with remarkable precision by a few rare mortals, while others required generations of inherited teaching. Seeker imagined that a created people might inherit a broader intuitive grasp of the relations between disciplines. Shaper envisioned a people capable of shaping and maintaining more sophisticated constructions than the world had ever held. Ascendant saw a chance for synthesis so great that its contribution could no longer be dismissed. Mortal collaborators imagined better infrastructure, safer protection, restoration of damaged living systems, and knowledge of the universe that might outlast any one god. They did not necessarily agree on why such beings should be made.

Their critics were not enemies of progress. Judge questioned what responsibility the creators would bear for risks imposed on autonomous lives. Guardian demanded that the people not be treated as expendable research. Warrior asked whether the makers would defend their freedom when military rulers wanted their abilities. Keeper warned that a powerful system of interacting magical affinities might contain instabilities no one would recognize until it had already become a society. Seeker insisted knowledge could avert catastrophe; Shaper argued that unrealized possibility also carried a moral cost; Ascendant objected to assumptions that its innovations were inherently irresponsible.

The seventh people's creation was the culmination of this civilization, not a sudden experiment in an empty laboratory. Seeker and Shaper had spent generations encouraging the study of relationships among the six magical disciplines. They imagined a new people able to perceive those relationships with extraordinary clarity, maintain structures too complex for ordinary institutions and discover ways to reduce the hazards of advanced magic. Ascendant saw an opportunity to contribute synthesis and amplification in a way even Shaper could not ignore. Mortal researchers wanted safer long-range passages, better protection, reliable healing and a deeper understanding of the world. Their motivations overlapped without being identical.

Judge questioned the right to bring into existence people whose unusual potential might threaten others, and the duty to protect their autonomy once they existed. Guardian asked whether the proposed people would be treated as free persons or as instruments for the ambitions of gods and scholars. Warrior feared that rulers would claim their talents as military property. Keeper demanded safeguards against a magical pattern that might have consequences beyond its designers' comprehension. The earlier unauthorized troll alterations made Keeper particularly reluctant to accept assurances from Ascendant. The project therefore reopened an old wound before the first member of the new people was born.

Once created, the seventh people formed a civilization of their own. They were not inherently evil, corrupted or obedient to their makers. Some pursued knowledge, others lived ordinary lives, and their growing independence forced the Seven to confront a moral fact: the makers could no longer simply cancel the project without doing violence to living people. The final name, cities and ultimate fate of the vanished people remain unconfirmed. The source decisions explicitly establish that Nhal's hidden corruption of their creation was central to the divine dispute and that the people vanished during the Divine War.

They created the **vanished seventh people**. Their proper name remains open: the formerly proposed “Aethren” was dropped because of its resemblance to “Aethyr.” The people were neither weapons nor predetermined servants of Nhal. They formed institutions and culture of their own, built cities, loved, fought, disagreed and made discoveries that even their creators had not anticipated. Their unusual magical potential lay not simply in huge reserves but in recognizing complex patterns among disciplines and stabilizing extraordinary workings. They became a civilization and not an experiment that could simply be switched off.

But the deepest achievements began to probe the conditions of reality itself. In the leading proposed causal sequence, a researcher trying to stabilize an extraordinary magical connection perceived a relation that no known discipline could fully explain. The insight produced a real improvement. It could be repeated and studied. Its success made further exploration inevitable. Yet it disturbed the underlying separation between the physical universe and the Void.

In the proposed sequence, the first opening was disguised as a useful discovery. A researcher attempting to stabilize an unusually sophisticated Form-and-Passage relation received an insight that seemed to arise from study. The technique worked. It spread through scholarly exchange because sharing reproducible knowledge was one of the Age of Wonders' great virtues. Only later did a second investigator perceive a troubling asymmetry: the working seemed to be observed from a direction no physical geometry could describe. Some scholars dismissed the observation as a Mind effect; others treated it as evidence of an unknown property of magic.

Nhal did not need to give them a false result. A true result was more persuasive. Through rare disturbances at the boundary of reality, Nhal could selectively supply insight and encourage repetition, but it could not simply walk into the world or control every mortal mind. A few investigators recognized a source beyond their normal understanding and began seeking it deliberately. These willing researchers became the ancestors of The Unmade. Many others had no knowledge that an external will was involved.

**Nhal noticed.**

Nhal could not enter. At first the influence was little more than observation through an extraordinary resonance. Repeated boundary-straining research allowed selective fragments of information to reach exposed minds. Nhal supplied genuine knowledge, but not its full costs. Researchers took apparent inspiration for their own genius. Others suspected an external will; a few sought it deliberately, hoping to go beyond the Seven's limits. The first cells that would eventually become **The Unmade** emerged as scattered circles of willing experimenters, secret custodians and ambitious collaborators embedded inside otherwise legitimate institutions. This was their origin: not an army from the Void, but mortals manipulating physical reality on behalf of the presence imprisoned beyond it.

Nhal retained knowledge the Seven lacked. It remembered the power that had cast it out, recognized them as Aethyr's fragments, and understood they were most dangerous when cooperative. Rather than overpower them, it appealed separately to each god's existing beliefs. Seeker heard the call of discovery; Shaper feared for created lives; Judge encountered evidence demanding accountability; Guardian saw dangers demanding sacrifice; Keeper found reason to conceal; Warrior detected dishonour; Ascendant saw old hostility vindicated. No god was a helpless puppet. Nhal did not possess universal mind control. It exploited genuine choices and used mortal followers to falsify archives, redirect inquiries and manipulate evidence.

An investigation into the seventh people's most advanced work uncovered real problems. Some involved methods developed by Ascendant. Because of the earlier troll creation breach, Keeper was legitimately suspicious and Judge legitimately concerned. Nhal's human and nonhuman agents—none of whom could openly identify themselves as serving the Void—mixed authentic technical problems with false evidence suggesting that Ascendant had intentionally corrupted the seventh people. The most effective lie was one built around something already true.

The Seven fractured over both facts and principles. Shaper refused to dismantle what might destroy a free civilization; Guardian insisted its people must be protected; Seeker wanted unrestricted examination; Keeper feared that publishing the dangerous mechanism would spread it; Judge demanded common rules; Warrior demanded honourable process; Ascendant withheld information and saw persecution. Every temporary restriction invited disobedience. Every disobedience convinced its opponents that restriction had been necessary. Mortal orders attempted to maintain contact, but courts, temples and armies chose conflicting patrons and causes.

The conflict was made possible by an accusation that combined genuine misconduct with manipulated evidence. Keeper's earlier experience of Ascendant's secrecy made suspicion reasonable; Ascendant's refusal to disclose everything made it worse. Judge wanted accountability, while Seeker demanded that unexplained anomalies be investigated before blame was assigned. Shaper feared destroying the very people the gods had created. Guardian feared for those people's lives, and Warrior objected to condemning anyone without honourable process. Nhal's mortal adherents altered testimony and presented carefully chosen fragments so that each god saw evidence confirming an existing fear.

A research restriction looked to one faction like necessary protection and to another like political suppression. Sheltering threatened scholars looked like mercy to Shaper and obstruction to Judge. Guardian's willingness to intervene outside the council looked like devotion to the innocent and an alarming precedent. Keeper's silence was intended to prevent replication of dangerous techniques but made independent verification harder. Seeker's refusal to stop investigating was an act of intellectual responsibility to some and recklessness to others. Ascendant's old envy became defensive secrecy. Their disagreements were sincere; Nhal's achievement was to make reconciliation appear impossible.

The **Divine War** unfolded through shifting allegiances, not two neat sides corresponding to modern Covenant and Dominion. Passage networks used for scholarship and trade became military roads. Ancient protective structures were attacked to capture cities. Fragile infrastructure broke; food failed to arrive; underground systems collapsed; whole populations fled. The war killed practitioners who carried irreplaceable skills and destroyed libraries before records could be duplicated. Mortals entered fights for religious principles, strategic land, retaliation or survival, often without knowing the gods' real arguments.

Keeper gradually recognized a pattern that ordinary damage could not explain: certain failures were being driven by pressure originating **outside** creation. Keeper did not possess a complete memory of Aethyr or know every detail of Nhal, but came to understand that a persistent alien intelligence was exploiting the war and that a sufficiently large breach might permit lasting incarnation.

Keeper's secrecy now worked against the warning. Judge wanted proof; Seeker wanted to reproduce observations; Shaper feared the proposed containment would annihilate the seventh people; Warrior would not abandon protections owed to innocents; Ascendant distrusted its accuser. Guardian eventually accepted Keeper's evidence and urgency. They faced a question with no clean answer: if containing the threat required destroying the structures sustaining millions, who could authorize that sacrifice?

The leading **working interpretation of the Great Betrayal** is that Guardian and Keeper acted without universal consent. Keeper conceived an extraordinary sealing of the reality boundary, using ancient distributed magical infrastructure as an anchoring framework. Guardian provided protective power. But these structures belonged to societies and other divine responsibilities. Appropriating them secretly was a breach of trust, even if the danger was real. Nhal exploited instability in the operation, magnifying the catastrophe as the seal drove its influence back.

The ensuing **Second Sundering** shattered the ancient central lands, ruined cities, deactivated or destroyed the great passage networks, transformed divine relationships and removed the seventh people from known history. The modern **Shrouded Lands** are a scar of this disaster, inaccessible by ordinary travel. The act may have saved all physical existence, but survivors could sincerely remember it as divine treachery. The precise mechanism, those culpable, and the final fates of the gods and lost people remain author questions rather than fixed canon.

The collapse was uneven. That inequality explains why modern peoples retain different amounts of ancient knowledge. A city sustained by far-reaching passageways could perish when those connections vanished. A defensible metropolis supplied by stone roads, deep wells and local craft could survive while its outskirts crumbled. Libraries and workshops disappeared; masters died; surviving wielders suppressed some specific advanced dangerous techniques, while others fled, hid their teachings or taught new royal institutions. Some reconstructed knowledge incorrectly. **Exactly one original great magical order survived secretly**, though its identity has not been selected. Modern academies range from direct lineages to fragments and recent inventions. Their competition over recovered knowledge became intense long after the ancient cooperation was forgotten.

At the same time the first **Guardians** arose from witnesses who grasped that some external influence had contributed to the war. Their task was not to govern nations or replace the great orders. It was to preserve selected warnings and dangerous evidence sufficient to recognize another attempt at entry. Their secrecy and reluctance to share information were rational responses to a world that had weaponized scholarship, but that secrecy would become a weakness when later rulers needed genuine answers.

The Guardians' origin belongs to the Divine War itself. In the working narrative they began as scholars and witnesses who found evidence that the gods' conflict had been influenced from outside creation. They gathered testimony, protected dangerous documents and developed ways of recognizing anomalous research without publicly reproducing it. They were not an offshoot of the one surviving great magical order by default, and did not form a third public state. Their duty was preservation and covert intervention; their flaw was the assumption that withholding truth would always keep it safe.

The Unmade's origin was earlier: certain scholars of the vanished people's era had deliberately followed the intelligence behind their discoveries. Their inner circles served Nhal knowingly; other adherents sought privilege, power or forbidden knowledge without grasping the ultimate purpose. The Second Sundering broke the ancient workings through which direct communication had been possible. For a very long period **the Unmade had no reliable contact with Nhal**. Its cells preserved instructions, distorted rituals and artifacts, but received no continuing stream of orders. Some cells disappeared; others embedded descendants in courts, academies, guilds, armies and religious institutions, waiting for the conditions that might restore contact.

The Guardians likewise survived by deep concealment, and their separate cells could disagree about how much truth to reveal. The original surviving great magical order remained a third, distinct institution: exactly one of the three or four old orders secretly survived, but its original mission, means of survival, archives and modern relationship with the Guardians were explicitly left unanswered in Q436–444. The narrative must not quietly assign those choices to a named Order of Preservation or give it omniscient control of the world.

The earliest **Unmade** circles suffered a catastrophe of their own. Their ability to communicate with Nhal depended on ancient research and deep magical workings that the Second Sundering destroyed, disabled or sealed. **Their contact with Nhal ceased.** No continuing guidance could be assumed. A surviving agent might have a written instruction, a remembered phrase, or a pattern whose original context was lost, but there were no regular new commands from the Void. This change is essential to the order's history: for generations and then centuries, the descendants of Nhal's collaborators became custodians of silent promises.

Some believed Nhal had abandoned them. Others believed the silence was evidence that the sealing worked and must eventually be undone. Some preserved authentic diagrams, some miscopied them, and some transformed fragments of research into dogma. Entire cells died trying to recreate contact prematurely. Others learned patience, embedding themselves in restored academies, temples, courts and commercial systems, hoping to gain access to surviving artifacts when the conditions changed. The Unmade thus survived without being a perfectly coordinated omnipotent conspiracy. Its inner leadership preserved knowing devotion to Nhal, while outer adherents sometimes saw themselves as elite scholars, patriots or reformers.

Outside those secret lineages lay the devastated world. Refugees crossed functioning ancient passages while they lasted, trekked across territories whose old borders had vanished, and built temporary settlements beside rivers and defensible ridges. The divine catastrophe had not destroyed all places equally. People who knew how to feed themselves without ancient infrastructure survived differently from those whose economies had been built around it.

Human communities re-established themselves across the **great inland plains**, where farmland, rivers, roads and administrative institutions allowed the gradual formation of a unified kingdom. Their old settlements did not all disappear; some were repaired, others abandoned, and new towns developed around safer water and transport. Their human racial capital became a postwar inland centre of monarchy, not the later alliance capital. Other human centres served river trade, frontier defence and magical scholarship.

**Dwarves** possessed an immense advantage and a serious burden: their ancestral mountain/subterranean metropolis **survived the Divine War**. It held genuine old structures, but not complete instructions for their operation. Some holds retained their works; others were cut off when deep routes failed, forcing migration through newly developed surface roads. Some halls were sealed for safety and others emptied. The dwarf confederation retained local hold sovereignty even as shared military and engineering obligations grew. Volcanic forging settlements, a coastal harbour stronghold and a monumental stone surface city represented different postwar adaptations, not replacements for the ancient surviving capital.

**Orcs** rebuilt and expanded from their ancestral **grasslands**, preserving Great Clan identities, sacred lands, professional armies, champions and political councils. Families from displaced clans settled under the protection of relatives or allied Great Clans, while disputes over routes, grazing, farming and military obligations became part of later politics. Their orcish racial capital was a postwar grassland centre; sacred, military and coastal cities exercised different influences.

**Halflings** retained their inheritance from **underground valleys**. Some ancestral settlements and complex subterranean systems endured, some became dangerous and were sealed, and others were evacuated as old routes or water systems failed. Their migration toward orcish lands was shaped by a serious crisis whose exact cause remains open. The Great Clans offered sanctuary rather than making the halflings subordinates. Halfling guilds contributed arcane engineering, supply organization, transport mechanisms and defensive expertise. A modern surface racial capital and regional guild, manufacturing and internal-Dominion trade centres arose without obliterating their underground ancestry.

This cooperation formed the original core of **Dominion**. Orc military systems and halfling guild institutions grew into a sovereignty-respecting alliance whose High Ruler would be selected through trials involving champions nominated by four member civilizations. The enormous **Dominion capital** rose in the **highlands**, deliberately separate from the orcish grassland racial capital. Leadership was earned and recognized; no single Great Clan became permanent monarch over the other peoples.

**Humans and dwarves**, meanwhile, cooperated because food, metal, engineering and defence connected their recovery. Surface human agricultural routes supported mountain cities while dwarven fortifications protected human settlements. A royal pact became the basis of **Covenant**, protecting the sovereignty of dwarven holds and the human kingdom. The Covenant High Ruler was drawn from a **separate branch of the human royal dynasty**, while the human monarch ruled the inland-plains kingdom. The huge mixed-race **Covenant capital** emerged on the **coast** at a junction of allied routes, distinct from the human capital. The formation of Covenant and Dominion followed the Divine War by long mortal histories; they were not two direct continuation armies of the gods.

Two further divisions made the alliances emotionally impossible to untangle.

The original Guardian-created **elven civilization** did not immediately become two species. A dispute over magical **responsibility** grew: some argued the lost age demonstrated the danger of unchecked research; others believed the suppression of knowledge had helped make ancient catastrophe unavoidable. As the disagreement became religious and political, the **original elven capital was destroyed**. Exactly how is still undecided; an experimental disaster and civil war were explored but never selected as a definitive cause. Those who became **Aelari** established a woodland monarchy in primeval forest, worshipped Guardian and preserved Life, wards and ancestral spellcraft. Those who became **Vaelari** established an arcane-led mountain-lake capital, worshipped Seeker and prized research, magical perception and advanced combinations. Aelari aligned with Covenant, Vaelari with Dominion, carrying incompatible memories of the lost city and accusations into the war. Their modern rulers are **Queen Seralyth** and **First Archon Selrion**. An earlier suggestion that they had been close friends is only workable if historically before strict separation, and is not confirmed.

The original **troll civilization** likewise had one ancestry and two co-creator traditions. The **Korrath** retained the ancestral **surviving highland capital**, councils of elders, sacred Keeper worship, old wards and chronicles. A population that revered Ascendant and emphasized growth and adaptation was **exiled**; it became the **Vorrath**, establishing a sacred **storm-coast capital** under religious leadership. The exact precipitating event is unresolved; competing claims of dangerous rituals, religious repression and falsified chronicles remain narrative possibilities. First Elder **Dromak** is a confirmed Korrath leader name; **Vashara** is a proposed Vorrath High Oracle. Korrath joined Covenant and Vorrath Dominion, carrying the exile's injustice and fear into different sides of a permanent war.

These histories were not detached from material life. Religious conflict meant disputed ancestral libraries, territory, pilgrimage roads and old monuments. The loss of a river crossing could starve a region. A surviving dwarf mechanism could be a national treasure, a dangerous unknown, or a strategic weapon. A halfling guild's recovered transport technique could sustain an army. Old elven manuscript claims could lead to raids on a ruin. Korrath and Vorrath chronicles could contradict the account used to justify exile. Each culture's relationship with the Divine War was alive in local government, supply routes, worship and family story.

Generations of conflict turned those grievances into an enduring **Covenant–Dominion war**. Each alliance possessed legitimate fears and remembered genuine harms; neither was wholly righteous or intrinsically evil. There was no normal friendly cross-alliance citizenship, trade, diplomacy or military service. Yet war required contact of another kind. Naval expeditions crossed seas. Military landing garrisons occupied enemy coasts. Scouts and patrols traveled roads used by local civilians. Fortified mines, salvage stations and research camps competed for scarce resources and artifacts. A Covenant fort on Dominion's continent remained an enemy occupation; a Dominion resource outpost in Covenant's woodland or mountains remained a hostile foothold, not a shared city.

Old trade patterns helped make those incursions possible. A road formerly used to move grain or ore became a military supply corridor; an old harbour that once served artisans became a naval landing; a forgotten valley pass held ruins valuable to archaeologists and generals alike. This did not restore normal trade between alliances: it explains how geography and infrastructure were repurposed by war. Smugglers, bandits, military requisition officers and profiteers existed within faction worlds and around borders, but any clandestine dealings with the opponent were exceptional and dangerous. Artifacts mattered not only because they were powerful. Some offered knowledge of material transformation, defences, healing, long-range Passage, or stabilization no modern nation possessed. Rulers funded expeditions in hopes of military advantage; honest scholars wanted to understand them; the Guardians feared uncontrolled boundary contact; the Unmade searched for components required to restore Nhal's connection.

To preserve player progression and meaningful danger, overseas outposts should be distributed across **level-appropriate frontier areas** rather than placed only in endgame zones. Early reconnaissance leads to raids on caravans, resource control, excavations, stronger fortress assaults and eventually major strategic installations. Players undertaking quests might collide with opponents protecting the same supplies. Enemy guards and patrols provide danger even without enemy players. The working example **Broken Coast** on Dominion land includes a Covenant beachhead, Dominion garrison and contested ore operations; **Ashen Reach** on Covenant land contains a Dominion landing, Covenant hill fort and quarry. The exact names and boundaries are not final. The **Shrouded Lands** remain inaccessible through ordinary travel until ancient Passage routes are unlocked for later larger-scale contested PvP.

The Guardians and Unmade exploited this same geography for very different reasons. A Guardian cell might send a native Covenant operative to recover a dangerous item from a Covenant expedition overseas, while a Dominion Guardian agent separately tracked an Unmade infiltrator in Dominion institutions. The two operatives served a single clandestine protective purpose without public alliance cooperation. An Unmade agent inside a garrison might secure an artifact by persuading a loyal officer it was strategically vital. The garrison would remain publicly loyal to its proper alliance. Thus the same outposts could host military missions, archaeological quests and deep secret-war stories across many regions and character levels.

The attempted peace was difficult because each alliance's internal politics carried different burdens. Covenant's hereditary High Ruler had to preserve the confidence of a human monarchy, independent dwarven holds, Aelari woodland rulers and Korrath elders. Dominion's trial-elected High Ruler answered to Great Clans, halfling guilds, Vaelari arcane institutions and Vorrath religious authorities. The negotiations were therefore a rare political exception, not a return to ordinary cross-alliance trade or diplomacy.

The confirmed modern leaders **Queen Seralyth** of Aelari, **First Elder Dromak** of Korrath and **First Archon Selrion** of Vaelari carried inherited schisms into their governments. The additional proposed cast—Covenant High Ruler Alaric Veyne, human Queen Elowen Veyne, dwarf High Thane Bromund Stonevein, Dominion High Ruler Draven Korr, halfling Guildmaster Tessa Brindle and Vorrath High Oracle Vashara—provides useful personal motivations but is not yet user-approved by name. Alaric may fear sacrificing alliance unity for peace; Elowen may fear abandoning frontier dead; Bromund may resent wars draining defensive engineering; Dromak may protect chronicles whose disclosure could inflame religion. Draven's authority rests on trials and earned leadership, Tessa on guild livelihoods, Selrion on research, Vashara on exile and sacred memory. No ruler is automatically an Unmade agent merely because their policies are exploitable.

The Unmade chose the negotiations as a target precisely because genuine peace would reduce military secrecy, lower demand for desperate weapons and allow rulers to investigate anomalies that had been dismissed as enemy sabotage. Its agents operated through native institutions on both sides. The attack destroyed the delegation, planted conflicting evidence and made both populations believe their ancient enemy had betrayed a last opportunity for peace. This was the **Shattered Delegation**, whose Unmade authorship is established, while exact victims, weapon and venue remain proposed.

The Guardians' proposed response was fractured and painful. A cell may have recognized suspicious artifact transfers but lacked sufficient information to stop the attack. Afterwards Guardian operatives protected witnesses, searched records and debated whether revealing incomplete evidence would trigger purges or compromise their agents. Their covert channels could cross political lines, but public Covenant and Dominion forces never cooperated. The failure deepened the Guardians' internal argument over secrecy, even as war expenditure and military expeditions created more cover for the Unmade.

At one exceptional moment, exhausted Covenant and Dominion rulers contemplated **peace negotiations**. This was not normal diplomacy but a rare effort to interrupt generations of conflict. The negotiations promised to reduce casualties, redirect scarce resources and permit some societies to look beyond immediate military survival.

The Unmade feared that peace would destroy the conditions on which its survival plan relied. War kept rulers suspicious, justified dangerous research and concealed missing shipments, altered records and illegal archaeological work. Its secret agents **orchestrated the Shattered Delegation attack** and framed each alliance for the destruction. The exact weapon, attack leader, attendees and location remain working matters, but **The Unmade's authorship is confirmed**. Both Covenant and Dominion emerged certain the other had betrayed the attempt at peace. Losses were carried into families and governments. A possible group of mortal hardliners may have been recruited or manipulated, but the event was directed by the servants of Nhal, not by an independent cabal alone. The war hardened.

The Guardians suspected a concealed hand but lacked proof they could reveal without compromising their network. Some Guardians feared that public disclosure would simply cause purges or inspire rulers to exploit the forbidden magic uncovered. Others felt that concealment was repeating Keeper's failure. Their choice to remain hidden protected their members while enabling the Unmade's narrative to spread.

Then, after countless generations of silence, **Nhal began to be heard again**.

The same changing magical conditions awakening rare mortal potential also reactivated fragments of old infrastructure. A long-inert mechanism responded to a new wielder. An ancient device whose purpose no scholar remembered began producing subtle distortions. Some Unmade families possessed manuals warning that particular phenomena would precede restored contact. At first signals were sporadic: a suggestion, an improbable correct insight, a pattern recurring across independent experiments. The inner leadership did not immediately know whether this was Nhal or a trick perpetrated by rival Unmade cells. They tested it through increasingly dangerous magical workings.

Once responses became reproducible, the meaning was terrifying. The generations who had preserved silent rituals were no longer alone. Nhal's selective communication resumed—not with every cell and not constantly, but through certain rare resonances strengthened by the world's awakening. Inner Unmade leaders received direction after millennia of extrapolating from old fragments. Their preparations became coordinated in a way that had not been possible since the Second Sundering. Old caches and buried plans acquired meaning; agents within royal courts, guilds, militaries and academies were instructed to acquire components, obstruct investigators and accelerate research into ancient links.

**This is why The Unmade is an increasingly dangerous modern threat rather than an omnipotent ancient conspiracy that could have conquered the world at any time.** For most of the mortal age its supreme source of direction was silent. Now it is beginning to speak again. Its servants are more prepared than the Guardians realize, while Nhal is still constrained and not yet able to act freely.

The awakening itself is not evil. Major cities can benefit from more healers, better wards, revived engineering and more gifted citizens. Rural populations may gain abilities previously unavailable. Mythic creatures may return, some dangerous, others simply long absent. But instability near deep ancient structures can also expose vulnerable individuals to contact. Academies disagree over restriction versus experimentation. Militaries fund research for strategic advantage. Traditional engineers and magical scholars compete over which methods are safe and effective. This landscape is precisely what Nhal wants: rival institutions discovering powerful truths without sharing the warnings that would let them interpret those truths correctly.

A player enters Theia amid these consequences, not amid exposition about the two primordial powers. As a human, dwarf, Aelari or Korrath they belong permanently to **Covenant**; as an orc, halfling, Vaelari or Vorrath they belong permanently to **Dominion**. The opening story depends on their culture. A royal frontier garrison betrays a village's trust. Ancient dwarf mechanisms wake beneath surviving halls. Aelari sanctuary wards fail. Korrath wardstones cease to respond. Great Clans dispute a champion's honour. Halfling guild engineers expose sealed ancestral works. Vaelari research accidents threaten neighbours. Vorrath religious signs divide pilgrims. These are meaningful local dramas independent of Nhal.

As players explore, the alliance war brings them to enemy continental outposts and contested resources. The same military expedition may put them near a stolen relic that Guardian observers have been tracking for generations. An Unmade cell may appear at first to be an academic fraud, an ambitious supply officer, a religious zealot or a corrupt commander. The Guardian who first aids the player is not introduced as the member of a new playable political faction; that identity is revealed cautiously, because open affiliation could make the player's alliance accuse them of treason.

The launch story unfolds across roughly six **non-mandatory thematic level bands** toward the working cap of sixty: racial beginnings; regional exploration and early dungeons; military and overseas incursions; ancient magical awakening; exposure of Unmade infiltration; and the defeat of a dangerous Unmade attempt to create a persistent Void anchor. Each alliance has separate protagonists, quests, staging grounds and final operations. There is no mixed-alliance party or public reconciliation. Covenant and Dominion can independently disrupt parts of a common Unmade plan while remaining each other's enemy.

The level-sixty victory is consequential. The player helps defend their own alliance against an ancient working and defeats a senior Unmade operation. The Shattered Delegation's secret authorship may finally be revealed to selected characters, though not necessarily accepted by governments as public truth. An authentic ancient document hints that the Great Betrayal of the Divine War may have been intended to prevent something worse. The final raid concept, **The Severed Chain**, concerns severing the anchor's supports, not killing Nhal as an ordinary creature.

Beyond this first campaign lie the Shrouded Lands and the rediscovery of ancient Passage. The two alliances will enter separately and contest ruined territory. Lost documents may expose the manipulated accusation against Ascendant, the tragic truth of the vanished seventh people and the incomplete memory of the Seven. The surviving great magical order, Guardians and Unmade will compete over what is recovered. One potential endgame dilemma is whether to restore the catastrophic old seal, risk removing it, or compel the Seven to reunify into Aethyr—possibly destroying their independent selves. Another working possibility involves mortals maintaining a new boundary through complementary, independently operated mechanisms. **None of these endings is final canon.**

The world that Aethyr created through self-sacrifice has not yet understood the cost of that act. Its seven successors built lives and civilizations while their own virtues turned against one another. Nhal could not defeat Aethyr directly; it nearly triumphed by breaking the trust of what Aethyr left behind. The Unmade repeated the pattern with Covenant and Dominion, first preserving a devotion through centuries of silence, then manipulating fresh mortal grievances when their master finally began to whisper again.

That is the historical line linking creation, divine relationships, the seventh people, the Second Sundering, ancestral migrations, surviving cities, royal treaties, clan refuge, racial schisms, the Shattered Delegation, offshore occupations, ancient artifact hunts, the Guardians, The Unmade, and the opening experiences of players. None is an isolated lore event. Each is an inherited consequence of a world that has repeatedly tried to save itself without knowing whether the cost was shared justly.
