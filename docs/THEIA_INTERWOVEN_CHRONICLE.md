Before there was a world, there was no sky to divide above from below, no distance to cross, and no moment by which one event could be measured against another. Neither the Physical Realm nor the Void existed. There were no realms at all, no established laws of nature, and no boundary separating what could exist from what could not. Yet two primordial essences endured beyond every condition that later beings would call reality.

**Aethyr** was the supreme creative will: not merely a god of life or light, but the potential for existence itself, the power by which something might arise where nothing had been. **Nhal** was its opposite: not a creature of darkness dwelling in some already formed abyss, but the supreme will to negate, to undo whatever might be made, until even the possibility of its existence was extinguished. Their power was older and more fundamental than the gods who would one day emerge. Where Aethyr sought to bring forth creation, Nhal sought to unmake it.

Their struggle had no battlefield, because space did not yet exist. It had no beginning that could be recorded, because time had not yet begun. Later minds might describe it as lasting eons, but even that word borrows a measure from a universe that had not been born. Every expression of Aethyr's creative purpose met Nhal's opposing will; every act of unmaking was resisted by the possibility of becoming. Neither could overcome the other. Their contest settled into an immeasurable stalemate, and no creation could be assured of enduring while its destruction remained within Nhal's reach.

In that impasse Aethyr conceived a different kind of victory. To defeat Nhal outright had proved impossible. But perhaps Nhal did not need to be destroyed. If creation could be given a realm of its own, and Nhal confined to another realm from which it could not freely reach what Aethyr made, existence might finally be allowed to unfold. The answer was not greater force in the old struggle; it was to establish the very conditions under which that struggle could no longer proceed as before.

The cost was Aethyr itself.

Aethyr understood that creating such a separation would require an act beyond ordinary creation: the breaking of its own primordial unity. The power that had resisted Nhal for an immeasurable existence would be spent in a single deliberate sacrifice. Aethyr would not remain whole to govern the result, to witness its future, or to defend each new life personally. Yet if the plan succeeded, what came afterward would have something Aethyr had never been able to guarantee—**the chance to exist without being immediately unmade**.

And so Aethyr chose.

In an instant beyond the measure of any clock, Aethyr shattered itself.

This was the **First Sundering**. The eruption of primordial energy did not merely fill an empty universe; it **brought two distinct realms into existence**. One was the **Physical Realm**, in which space, time, matter and the fundamental laws of magic could arise and endure. The other was the **Void**, not a second world of mountains and cities, but a realm of negation beyond the conditions of ordinary physical existence.

The force of the shattering drove Nhal into the newly formed Void. Aethyr's own shattered energies passed into the Physical Realm. In its final creative act, Aethyr established a **Veil between the two realms**—a fundamental separation intended to prevent Nhal from reaching across and unmaking the existence now taking shape.

It was not a prison built around an existing abyss. The prison, the world it protected, and the boundary between them were consequences of the same sacrifice. **Aethyr had changed the terms of the primordial conflict by creating the separation that neither essence had previously possessed.**

Nhal survived. Its nature was primordial and could not be extinguished merely by being cast beyond the new physical order. But survival was not freedom. Nhal was confined beyond the Veil, unable simply to reach into the Physical Realm and erase whatever emerged there. Aethyr's purpose had been achieved, at a price no later god could fully comprehend.

The Veil had denied Nhal the power to intervene, **not the ability to observe**. From its confinement Nhal could witness the Physical Realm unfold: the Seven awakening, their differing ambitions, the emergence of mortal races and the increasingly intricate workings of magic. It could learn from what it perceived, but it could not reach across, change a mortal's thoughts, alter a spell, send a warning or touch the smallest stone. Its perception was not omniscience; hidden motives and future choices remained uncertain. For ages it watched Aethyr's fragments build a world it could not unmake. This long enforced observation would later give Nhal knowledge of the ambitions it might exploit, should any means of influence ever become possible.

Aethyr, as a single consciousness, was gone.

Within the Physical Realm, seven great concentrations of its shattered essence awakened as independent beings. They inherited extraordinary power, but not the whole mind, memory or intention of the one who had sacrificed itself. They did not know the long struggle, the revelation that had ended it, or the reason the Veil existed. None was appointed to rule the others. These seven would become known as **the Seven**.

And so creation began with a paradox that would haunt every later age: the first act that made lasting life possible was an act of self-destruction, undertaken to prevent a still-living enemy from destroying all that might follow. The gods would inherit the world made by that choice without understanding its price. Nhal, beyond the Veil, would remember what they had forgotten.

The exact physics of the Veil, the means by which later extraordinary magic might disturb it, and whether the primordial separation could ever be permanently repaired remain questions for the larger saga. But the reason for its creation was clear: **Aethyr chose sacrifice so that creation could finally have a future.**

In the ages that followed, the Seven felt a strange familiarity with one another but could not explain it. No one was their king. They discovered their powers gradually, learned magical laws rather than inventing them, and developed independent personalities, ambitions and loyalties.

The seven did not awaken together. They emerged into the young Physical Realm at different moments and came to know themselves before they knew one another. No god was appointed sovereign over the others; no voice explained that they were fragments of Aethyr. The order of their individual awakenings remains partly unrecorded in the creator's history. What mattered was that relationships formed through experience rather than being inherited fully made.

Among the earliest and most consequential was the bond between **Guardian and Warrior**. It did not begin in agreement. The young realm was still turbulent, its first enduring formations threatened by the forces that shaped them. Guardian regarded their fragility as a reason to intervene; Warrior questioned whether constant shelter could produce anything capable of standing on its own. Guardian answered that a thing destroyed before it had a chance to grow could never learn strength. Warrior argued that strength granted entirely by another's intervention was not strength discovered for oneself. Neither wanted pointless destruction. Neither was prepared to surrender the principle at stake.

The precise first encounter remains open to literary refinement, but the course of their relationship is established. They returned to the disagreement, observed the consequences of their choices and slowly learned to respect what the other understood. Guardian came to recognize the dignity of resilience and independent will. Warrior came to see that protection could make strength possible rather than diminish it. Disagreement became conversation, conversation became cooperation, and cooperation became the closest friendship among the Seven. They would not always agree—especially when honour and sacrifice later came into conflict—but they learned to trust that the other acted from a serious conviction.

Elsewhere, **Seeker** investigated the patterns underlying the newborn realm. The existence of matter and energy did not explain why particular forms endured or why a magical arrangement responded to intention. Seeker learned to distinguish reproducible law from coincidence and to value an unanswered question more than an elegant but untested certainty. **Shaper**, working independently, approached the same world from the opposite direction. Where Seeker wished to understand a phenomenon, Shaper wished to make something with it. Shaper's first works were experiments in form, construction and transformation, each one an argument that reality could become more than it had been.

Their meeting began with an anomaly. In this working dramatic account, Seeker encountered one of Shaper's formations and found that it remained stable despite violating an accepted model. Shaper did not know the full reason it worked; Seeker had not imagined that arrangement. Seeker proposed an adjustment and Shaper gave it form. A structure that had survived by inspired trial became something they could understand, refine and reproduce. Then Shaper tried a variation Seeker had not anticipated and exposed another principle. Each discovered that the other's gift could carry their own further than solitary work.

The collaboration grew into affection and intellectual trust. Seeker admired Shaper's courage to attempt the seemingly impossible. Shaper admired Seeker's ability to turn invention into dependable knowledge. Their investigations transformed the young Physical Realm: stable structures, carefully governed energies and increasingly sophisticated patterns became possible. Keeper would later recognize that the stability of such works depended on rules neither partner always respected; Judge would see that powerful creators needed responsibilities as well as freedom. Yet these concerns had not yet broken the extraordinary confidence of the early age.

The first truly transformative question emerged from Seeker and Shaper's work: **could the principles that sustained a magical form also permit an independent living consciousness?** Not merely a moving construct, nor a servant echoing a maker's intentions, but a being able to experience the world, choose, learn and become something its creator had never planned.

Seeker and Shaper made the foundational discovery that independent mortal life was possible. But possibility was not proof of safety. They could model complex self-sustaining patterns and describe the conditions under which individuality might arise, yet they could not confidently establish what would separate a free consciousness from an extension of divine will, or how such a being could endure without constant intervention. **They chose not to attempt mortal creation yet.** This was a deliberate pause because their knowledge remained incomplete, not a failure of imagination. Seeker believed further understanding was owed to any life they might bring forth. Shaper accepted the pause but felt the frustration of seeing an extraordinary possibility left unrealized.

Their findings reached Guardian and Warrior, who saw in them questions different from those the two researchers had asked. Guardian wanted life that could flourish for its own sake, even if its maker could not predict what it would become. Warrior wanted life that could choose, strive and overcome difficulty through its own will. Their old disagreement—protection against the danger of overprotection—became the source of two complementary approaches to the unresolved problem. Rather than wait for a complete theoretical account, they took Seeker and Shaper's research further through their own understanding of living independence and will.

**Guardian created the elves, the first of the mortal races.** They were not instruments fashioned to worship or obey. Their capacity for life, relationship and adaptation gave them a future their creator could nurture but not wholly command. **Warrior created the orcs**, likewise free to grow beyond their maker's expectations, with the potential for endurance, courage, communal honour and the strength to face adversity. Their creations followed close enough in the earliest history that each god could witness the other's achievement. Neither regarded the other people as a failed version of its own. For Guardian and Warrior, the difference between elves and orcs confirmed the lesson their friendship had taught them: independent life could flourish through more than one set of virtues.

The new races changed the other gods as profoundly as they changed the world. Their lives revealed that the creation of a person was not the completion of a work but the beginning of a history. They made choices, formed bonds and altered their surroundings. Even their divine makers were surprised. Guardian learned the pain of watching a beloved creation risk harm. Warrior discovered that honour freely chosen by mortals meant more than honour simply imposed. Seeker found a new field of inquiry in the unpredictable development of mortal minds. Shaper watched beings transform the world around them and saw the possibility of creators who were themselves creations.

**Shaper then created the dwarves.** Inspired by the capacity of elves and orcs to act independently, Shaper envisioned people who would not merely inhabit a formed world but transform it through craft, construction and enduring works. Their aptitude for making was not a command to reproduce divine designs. It was an invitation to surpass them. When dwarves began inventing forms and techniques Shaper had not taught, the god experienced an extraordinary pride. Mortal admiration for Shaper's visible contributions would grow from these achievements, later becoming one source of Ascendant's envy.

Seeker did not create a separate one of the original six mortal races. Instead, Seeker became increasingly fascinated by mortal discovery itself. The elves, orcs and dwarves could observe, test and challenge even divine explanations. Seeker's role as investigator and teacher would later make the god a principal patron of halflings and Vaelari, though neither people had been created by Seeker. The distinction between **creator** and **patron** began to matter long before the modern religions took shape.

As mortals formed relationships and communities, **Judge** confronted questions that had scarcely existed before free races appeared. What law could govern beings who could refuse their makers? How should competing claims be reconciled? What obligations did power create? Judge created **humans**, whose adaptability and capacity to form intricate institutions reflected those questions. Their later principal devotion to Guardian would show that an origin did not determine a people's permanent faith.

**Keeper** observed another aspect of mortality. A person could be free and still fragile. A community could flourish and still collapse when its sustaining patterns failed. Keeper studied how living systems could remain stable through change without being held motionless by a divine hand. From this concern came **halflings**, whose inherited magical resilience would later express itself in sophisticated guild traditions and engineering. Like humans, halflings would eventually choose a principal patron other than their original creator: Seeker.

By this point **five mortal races existed: elves, orcs, dwarves, humans and halflings**. Their early communities were developing identities, knowledge and relationships independent of their gods. The five creators and Seeker had shared discoveries, disagreements, celebrations and the first anxieties of responsibility. This was the divine family and its living legacy into which Ascendant would enter.

**Ascendant had not only just awakened.** He had spent a long period independently exploring the realm, developing an understanding of adaptation, amplification and synthesis, and shaping a philosophy that emphasized the potential to go beyond an original design. When he finally found the others, he encountered equals who nevertheless possessed a history together that he could not share. Their friendships were established. Their work was visible in five living races. The other gods did not have to reject him deliberately for their ease with one another to feel like exclusion.

He admired much of what they had made. He also wondered whether they had left room for anything truly new. Seeker and Shaper's celebrated collaboration suggested a place for insight and invention, but Shaper was already beloved for the dwarves and other great works. Guardian and Warrior had proved that life could arise from different philosophies. Judge and Keeper had each contributed a people. Ascendant had extraordinary power, but no comparable legacy and no people who knew him as their maker.

His desire to contribute could still have become an uncomplicated source of creativity. Instead, recognition slowly became entangled with identity. He wanted to demonstrate that the world could not merely be preserved, understood or shaped: it could be **improved beyond the limits its first makers had accepted**. Keeper possessed knowledge of magical stability that Ascendant's independent experiments could not fully reproduce. Their future collaboration in creating the trolls therefore offered both a practical partnership and a chance for a lasting legacy. The later Seventh project would carry that same desire to a far more dangerous scale.

None of this made Ascendant inherently evil, nor did it predetermine his response to Nhal. It made him a person whose genuine creative gift and growing need for acknowledgement could become inseparable. The tragedy of the later age would arise because other gods also possessed virtues capable of becoming dangerous when they stopped listening to one another.

Keeper and Ascendant had not been close friends before their collaboration. Ascendant had come late to the divine community, discovering five mortal races already alive and the other gods' achievements already celebrated. Keeper possessed knowledge Ascendant needed: the understanding of how to give living magical structures stability without extinguishing their independence. Ascendant offered a complementary gift, the capacity to amplify and adapt what already existed. Together they began creating the trolls, the sixth mortal people.

Keeper wished to give them a stable magical constitution and the ordinary capacity to endure the changing world. Ascendant contributed an adaptable nature, but privately wanted something more personal from this creation. He had seen elves honour Guardian, orcs revere Warrior and dwarves celebrate Shaper. The new people might finally be a living testament to his own significance. And so he considered a subtle alteration to the sensitivity to primordial energy that each created race inherited through its ancestry.

Through the creation process, **each mortal race inherited traces of Aethyr's primordial energy**, the same fundamental power from which the Seven themselves had emerged. As the gods shaped their creations, that energy manifested in different ways, giving rise to distinct racial affinities and magical potential—not fragments of the gods' personal essences. Over time, studying these manifestations would lead to the discovery and understanding of the **six fundamental fields of magic**. **Ascendant secretly amplified the trolls' sensitivity to this primordial energy and its divine resonance**, hoping they would feel the presence of divinity more keenly and, in time, choose to honour him above the others. He did not give them the ability to heal any wound, survive any blow or evolve new powers when threatened. The alteration concerned their **spiritual receptivity**, not their combat strength. It offered no guarantee of direct conversation with a god, superior spellcasting, exceptional magical reserves or obedience.

The trolls emerged as free mortal beings. They could be injured, fall ill, age and die, as other races could. Their heightened sensitivity to Aethyr's primordial energy could express itself through experiences that their descendants interpreted as spiritual, through devotion to ritual, and through an unusually rich religious imagination. But it could not choose their beliefs for them. A troll could question a divine teaching, reject a priest's interpretation or follow a different patron. A people created with heightened awareness was not a people created without free will.

Keeper eventually recognized that Ascendant had altered the agreed design. The immediate danger was not an army of invulnerable beings; it was the attempt of one creator to shape the religious inclinations of independent lives for personal recognition. Keeper had consented to a stable, resilient people, not to an undisclosed amplification of their sensitivity to the gods. Removing the alteration after the trolls existed risked interfering with living beings who had committed no wrong. Keeper chose to preserve them, but the decision could not restore trust.

Ascendant defended the work: the trolls remained free, and their heightened spiritual awareness could be a genuine gift. Keeper did not deny either point. What Keeper could not accept was that Ascendant had decided, alone, to change the conditions under which those beings would develop their beliefs. The distinction mattered. **Ascendant had not forced the trolls to worship him, but he had tried to influence the path by which they might come to do so.**

The irony would unfold across generations. Troll religious traditions flourished, but devotion did not converge upon Ascendant. Some communities revered the promise of change and growth associated with him. Others increasingly honoured Keeper's restraint, stability and guardianship. Ascendant had helped create a people unusually attentive to divine questions, only to discover that their independence included the freedom to prefer his collaborator. His desire for recognition had produced an achievement he could neither claim exclusively nor control.

This difference in worship would eventually contribute to the religious division between the **Korrath**, who retained the ancestral highland capital and chiefly honoured Keeper, and the **Vorrath**, who were exiled and principally revered Ascendant. Yet the alteration itself did **not** cause that exile. The schism arose much later through mortal religious institutions, political disputes, contested sacred sites and decisions made over generations. Neither branch was born corrupted, superior or destined for a particular allegiance.

Other gods could admire the trolls while condemning Ascendant's concealment. Judge would have reason to object to the violation of an agreed undertaking; Guardian to defend the independence of the new people; Seeker to question the ethics and interpretation of the alteration; Warrior to object to dishonour without blaming the innocent; and Shaper to recognize a real creative accomplishment without endorsing its means. These individual reactions remain literary possibilities rather than confirmed dialogue.

The consequences for Keeper and Ascendant were enduring. Keeper became more wary of undisclosed modifications to living magical structures, especially those that might influence the freedom of their inhabitants. Ascendant increasingly believed that his finest achievements would be judged by the manner of their making rather than their results. **When the proposal to create the seventh race arose much later, both would remember the trolls—but draw opposite lessons from them.**

Mortals were never confined to their creators' intentions. The orcs developed scholars as well as champions; dwarves developed healers as well as masons; elves could choose life without wielding magic. Racial bloodlines inherited divine essence, ordinarily subtle and occasionally concentrated through rare empowered ancestral lineages. Creator and chief patron were not synonyms. Humans created by Judge would eventually principally worship Guardian; halflings created by Keeper would turn toward Seeker. This pattern of changed allegiance later became central to the civilizational divisions.

The six races spread into their ancestral habitats. Human settlements occupied great inland plains and river valleys where agriculture could support population and law. Dwarven mountain holds dug immense subterranean works, protected mines and built stable roads and forges. Orcish Great Clans arose across grasslands, establishing religious centres, martial traditions and permanent cities. Halflings settled underground valleys, building intricate guild-based systems, waterways and mechanisms. Elves cultivated primeval forests and systems of living and magical stewardship. Trolls inhabited rugged highlands, where stone, defensive wards and chronicles preserved continuity. These places did not become six immutable racial theme parks: migration, geography and trade gradually established varied local cultures.

The gods were distant from daily mortal life. Their intervention was rare and significant. Much more commonly, mortal ingenuity solved mortal problems. Shaper was widely loved because cities and irrigation systems were visible achievements; Seeker found mortal discoveries exciting; Guardian protected races other than elves; Judge watched human institutions grow beyond its founding intentions. Ascendant noticed how rarely its improvements were credited, deepening envy without making the god naturally evil.

Mortals learned to work with **exactly six magical disciplines**: **Elements & Energy**, **Matter**, **Life**, **Form**, **Mind** and **Passage**. All races could study each; aptitude and ancestry affected affinity, not absolute permission. Magic drew on finite internal reserves guided by intent, skill and learned precision, recovered naturally and through ambient energy or disciplined meditation. Simple work might use one discipline; complicated effects several, which one practitioner had to understand personally. Several untrained mortals could not fuse into a master spellcaster. Extremely ambitious workings could overload concentration and go wrong; exhaustion normally prevented further casting. Stable matter created from nothing, self-sustaining enchantments, extraordinary resurrection and far-ranging passages became rare ancient arts, not simple conveniences.

The great magical orders translated exceptional knowledge into ordinary civic dependence. Their libraries preserved failed experiments as well as successes; their scholars crossed political boundaries, challenged gods openly and taught in independent institutions. Founding divine teachings, occasional direct instruction and trusted mortal intermediaries all played roles. An engineer might use a halfling regulator to maintain a dwarven defensive structure; an elven Life specialist could debate a human healer over the conditions of recovery; Passage experts could connect distant markets and archives. The great orders were three or four in number, independent yet interconnected, mostly cooperative until the war. Their exact names were never ratified.

The abundance of magical infrastructure had a hidden cost. A city that relied on ancient passages for grain could become vulnerable when the road beside it fell into disuse. A settlement protected by a self-sustaining ward might forget how to defend itself without that ward. A magical work maintained by an expert lineage could become irreparable when that lineage died. These dependencies help explain why the later catastrophe produced starvation, migrations and centuries of loss rather than a single dramatic night after which life simply resumed.

The Age of Wonders was therefore not an age of unlimited sorcery. It was an age of extraordinary accumulated knowledge. A single skilled engineer could maintain a city ward constructed by predecessors; a series of separate expert-created structures could sustain a long-range passage; a forged item could be charged and renewed. Practical magic and conventional craft supported one another even when their schools competed for recognition.

**Three or four great independent magical orders** emerged beside many smaller schools. Their identities came from philosophy, specialties and public missions, not a one-to-one correspondence with the six disciplines. They maintained shared libraries, visited one another, held scholarly councils and used the ancient passages. They received divine founding instruction, rare appearances and trusted intermediaries, yet could openly challenge divine ideas. They argued over ethical research, ownership of discoveries and political influence, but were mostly cooperative until war destroyed that arrangement. No final names or exact number beyond “three or four” have been approved. Exactly one original great order would secretly survive the catastrophe; which one remains undecided.

The greatest danger of the Age of Wonders did not begin as a council of seven gods agreeing to fashion a new people. It began with a private wound in the life of one god.

**Ascendant had never forgotten the trolls.** Their creation with Keeper had been a genuine achievement, and the trolls had become a free people with elaborate spiritual traditions. But Ascendant's concealed amplification of their sensitivity to divine essence had not produced the devotion he desired. Many honoured Keeper. Others honoured both creators, or drew their own conclusions about the gods. Ascendant had attempted to cultivate recognition without abolishing free will; their freedom had answered him in a way he could not control.

He had also learned something about Keeper. Beneath that god's caution lay an extraordinary command of how living magical patterns could endure without collapsing. Their estrangement was real, but Keeper did not wish every future exchange to be poisoned by the first betrayal. In the years that followed, Keeper cautiously permitted Ascendant to examine **limited portions of advanced stability research**. It was an attempt to restore a scholarly relationship under conditions both could understand. The permitted work concerned complex relationships among divine essence, living autonomy and the six disciplines. Certain incomplete experiments, whose effects Keeper could not explain or safely contain, remained restricted.

Ascendant accepted the arrangement. At first the exchange was honest. His insights into adaptation genuinely improved some of Keeper's theoretical models, and Keeper's methods corrected failures in Ascendant's own. But Ascendant noticed absences in the research: references without their underlying proofs, experiments whose conclusions were withheld, and patterns whose permitted forms implied more powerful variants. Keeper had drawn a boundary around knowledge for which no reliable safeguard yet existed. Ascendant began to regard that boundary as the very obstacle that had always prevented his genius from being recognized.

**Using the research Keeper had willingly granted him access to, Ascendant devised his own methods to reconstruct the gaps in the knowledge Keeper had deliberately withheld.** He then extended those recovered principles through his own mastery of amplification and synthesis. He knew he had exceeded the agreement. He did not tell Keeper. He was acting from conviction, wounded pride and the desire to create something undeniably his own.

His ambition had a precise object. The six original mortal races possessed distinct inherited affinities and could all learn magic through training, finite reserves, discipline and effort. Ascendant believed it possible to create a seventh race whose consciousness could apprehend the relationships among magical disciplines with an ease other mortals rarely achieved. Such beings would not possess limitless energy or immunity to death. They would still have to learn, practice, eat, build, argue and live. But they might see structures in Form and Passage that even accomplished scholars overlooked, and advance the arts of the Physical Realm far beyond their makers' expectations.

A second quality arose from the same radical design. The six original species could not ordinarily have children across species boundaries. **Ascendant's seventh race alone would be compatible with any of the six.** Whether he foresaw all the social consequences is not established; the compatibility expressed his central philosophy that creation should be able to cross limitations its predecessors accepted. 

Ascendant worked in secrecy, without the other gods contributing to the act of creation. He tested patterns, discarded failed structures and developed his own methods to combine the six magical fields with a precision previously unimagined in mortals. Many trials failed. Others succeeded because of his persistence and ingenuity. **Nhal neither guided these experiments nor contributed knowledge to the seventh race's creation.** The race's extraordinary aptitude was Ascendant's achievement, born of his determination to outshine the other gods. The unintended consequence was a possibility so remote that even Ascendant did not perceive it: in the hands of sufficiently gifted, trained and ambitious descendants, an exceptionally precise combination of all six fields might one day resonate with the Veil. This was not a flaw implanted by Nhal, an inevitable trait of every member of the race, or an open breach. It was an unrealized possibility within a creation whose potential exceeded its creator's foresight.

Beyond the Veil, Nhal watched. It had witnessed the birth of the first six races and the gods' growing relationships; now it saw Ascendant create beings capable of bringing the six expressions of Aethyr's primordial energy together at a depth mortals had never reached. Nhal recognized the significance because it remembered the primordial power that had formed its prison. But **recognition was not intervention**. It could not whisper to Ascendant, improve his results or change the seventh race's design. It could only hope that one day these new mortals would discover a configuration capable of disturbing the boundary.

Then the first of the seventh race awakened. The living consciousness was its own. Ascendant could not command its choices simply by having made it. More followed. They learned and formed attachments, developed preferences and disagreements, and began a history that did not belong to Ascendant alone. **He was their sole divine creator**, but he was not the author of every act they would undertake. At last he possessed a creation whose divine origin no other god could claim as shared labour.

He revealed them only after their independence and unusual aptitude could no longer be dismissed as speculation. Seeker was astonished by their capacity to understand magical relationships. Shaper admired the scale of Ascendant's accomplishment, even as the speed of its achievement unsettled old assumptions. Guardian saw free persons entitled to protection. Warrior respected their agency but warned that rulers might seek to possess their gifts. Judge questioned the secrecy of a creation whose consequences could affect every people. **Keeper recognized elements of research Ascendant had been forbidden to extend** and knew their agreement had been broken again.

Yet the other gods could not simply condemn the seventh race for their creator's conduct. They were alive, independent and, at first, flourishing. Keeper could identify unauthorized methods without proving that the new lives were themselves dangerous. Seeker insisted that a discovery could be examined without punishing its discoverers. Guardian refused to allow the people to become hostages in a dispute among gods. Ascendant heard the reservations as yet another refusal to acknowledge what he had accomplished.

The Seventh civilization grew around its own cities, workshops, archives and schools. Their scholars exchanged knowledge with the great magical orders; visiting researchers from the six older races carried home discoveries that changed healing, construction, defensive wards and Passage engineering. The new people were not one class of scholars or one mind. They raised families, worked fields, maintained buildings, governed disputes and questioned the promises made by their leaders. Their extraordinary potential required training and resources. 

For a time, the seventh race's achievements appeared to vindicate Ascendant. Their most celebrated research sought a lasting means of communicating with their divine creator. They had inherited a close relationship with complex magical structure, but **that was not the same as possessing an open path to the gods**. A stable channel to Ascendant might answer questions about their origin, permit divine instruction without rare manifestations and prove that mortals could build what previously only gods could sustain.

As generations passed, the seventh race's greatest researchers pushed beyond the boundaries of ordinary spellcraft. They did not know of the Veil or Nhal. Their studies concerned the six fields and the apparent unity of the primordial energy beneath them. An exceptionally difficult experiment brought all six into a fleeting configuration that **resonated with the Veil**. No doorway opened and no primordial corruption flooded through. Yet the resonance created the first minute opportunity for Nhal to act upon a working in the Physical Realm.

Nhal had already watched the researchers' methods, ambitions and desire to reach their divine creator. Now, through the specific resonance they had unwittingly produced, it could exert a **barely perceptible influence upon similar experiments**. It could not command minds, control all magic or appear as an openly speaking god. It could, however, favour one otherwise possible result over another, encourage an unstable pattern to settle, or allow an unexpectedly useful insight to arise from a repeated trial. These results were real enough to persuade scholars to investigate further. Only **after this first resonance** did Nhal begin to manipulate research. A few investigators noticed inconsistencies and quietly preserved evidence. Others mistook every improvement for their own success. A willing minority eventually suspected a hidden intelligence and pursued the source, laying the foundation for The Unmade.

Nhal did not foresee every step of what would follow. It recognized a possibility, cultivated it patiently and gradually redirected a legitimate project for divine communication toward a structure capable of widening the original resonance into a breach.

The great device they built would later be known as **the First Anchor**. Its public purpose was a permanent divine connection; its concealed architecture was being redirected by Nhal-influenced researchers toward the **Veil**, the primordial separation between the Physical Realm and the Void. The designers did not collectively intend to summon the destruction of their world. A willing minority concealed alterations, controlled records and steered the work toward an objective their colleagues did not understand. They were the earliest ancestors of **The Unmade**, although the later organization and its knowledge of Nhal would evolve over generations.

Other Seventh investigators noticed. They found discrepancies between approved designs and actual construction, missing experiment logs and signs that apparently independent discoveries had been steered toward the same dangerous outcome. They could not name Nhal. They could not even prove the unknown influence was a person rather than a magical phenomenon. They saved copies of evidence and tried to warn their colleagues. Their warnings collided with the prestige of a project that seemed to promise contact with their creator and with Ascendant's increasing refusal to believe that his people could be endangered by the very gift he had given them.

When the First Anchor was activated, the device established a connection—but not the one its builders had promised. It reached beyond the Physical Realm and **breached Aethyr's Veil**. The force pressing through was not merely an unusually large quantity of familiar magic. It was the intrusion of primordial unmaking into laws never intended to contain it. The Anchor became entangled with the rupture. Breaking the visible mechanism would not reliably close what had opened; it might tear away the remaining structure preventing an uncontrolled expansion.

**The first catastrophe of the Second Sundering began at the seventh race's homeland.** The land around the Anchor convulsed. Passage routes failed or turned unpredictable. Magical structures that had sustained cities began to collapse, and regions of the ancient world were torn apart or made unreachable. The seventh race's greatest centres suffered devastation. Other civilizations, dependent on the same networks for food, travel, protection and scholarship, experienced cascading failures. This was not the final extent of the world's losses; the Divine War and the later sealing would compound them.

Not all members of the seventh race were destroyed. Some died in the first rupture, some were twisted by the relentless corruption radiating from the damaged boundary, and some escaped into defended refuges. Others were already abroad, studying or trading with the six older races through the ancient Passage network. More fled during the interval before The Binding. In the devastated homeland, small groups would eventually preserve **multiple independent fortified enclaves**, neither a single hidden kingdom nor an immortal race. They survived through ancient wards, agriculture, maintenance, sacrifice and continual defence. Their corrupted kin were not automatically conscious members of The Unmade; some would retain broken memories of guarding ancestral sites and later reclaim ruins and strongholds without understanding why.

Outside the homeland, the surviving Seventh researchers faced a different danger. As stories spread that their civilization had broken the world, rulers and survivors sought culprits. Some wanted the seventh race's knowledge seized, others destroyed. The surviving scholars who had questioned the Anchor carried evidence that the research had been deliberately manipulated, but still did not know **Nhal's name or primordial origin**. They sought allies among the six older races, whose cities and magical institutions had also been wounded by the catastrophe.

**The Divine Schism grew in the wake of the rupture.** Keeper recognized the forbidden stability principles used in Ascendant's creation and demanded the full research. Ascendant, grieving for the people whose homeland lay ruined, believed the other gods were using the disaster to condemn his greatest achievement. Judge sought accountability; Guardian insisted on the rights of surviving innocents; Warrior opposed collective punishment and demanded honest judgment. Seeker believed the cause must be understood before knowledge was suppressed. Shaper feared that destroying every surviving advanced structure would destroy what little remained of civilization. These were not neat factions of good gods and evil gods. Each position carried genuine obligations and genuine dangers.

Nhal's willing followers took advantage of the conflict. They mixed authentic evidence of Ascendant's deception with forged claims that he had intentionally designed a weapon for unmaking. They presented incomplete records to gods already suspicious of one another. Ascendant concealed more of his work, thereby making honest scrutiny harder. Keeper withheld dangerous technical details for fear of enabling another Anchor. Seeker demanded access precisely because secrecy prevented verification. The first acts of repression produced resistance, and resistance seemed to vindicate repression. Mortal kingdoms, academies and orders took sides for reasons that included belief, survival, land, honour and the control of resources.

It was **during the Divine War, before The Binding**, that the first Guardians formally emerged. Seventh investigators who had preserved evidence of an unknown manipulation joined trusted scholars, witnesses and protectors from **the other six races**. Some Seventh founders had been abroad at the time of the Anchor's activation; others had escaped while the homeland remained accessible. Their coalition agreed to protect the evidence, investigate the influence without widely reproducing dangerous techniques, and prevent any god or state from monopolizing the truth. They did **not** yet know Nhal's identity. The Guardians were multiracial from their founding, and membership was defined by mission and trust rather than bloodline. They established cells and hidden repositories both inside and outside the region that would later become the Shroud.

The Unmade and Guardians therefore shared a historical crisis but not an identity. The Unmade grew from researchers who chose to follow the influence behind the Anchor. The Guardians grew from those who tried to expose and contain it, together with allies among the older races. The Seventh remained a **race**, not an organization: most of its survivors belonged to neither order. The Guardians' future secrecy and The Unmade's infiltration would become competing legacies of the same broken age.

The **Divine War** unfolded through shifting allegiances, not two neat sides corresponding to modern Covenant and Dominion. Passage networks used for scholarship and trade became military roads. Ancient protective structures were attacked to capture cities. Fragile infrastructure broke; food failed to arrive; underground systems collapsed; whole populations fled. The war killed practitioners who carried irreplaceable skills and destroyed libraries before records could be duplicated. Mortals entered fights for religious principles, strategic land, retaliation or survival, often without knowing the gods' real arguments.

Keeper gradually recognized a pattern that ordinary damage could not explain: certain failures were being driven by pressure originating **outside** creation. Keeper did not possess a complete memory of Aethyr or know every detail of Nhal, but came to understand that a persistent alien intelligence was exploiting the war and that a sufficiently large breach might permit lasting incarnation.

Keeper's secrecy now worked against the warning. Judge wanted proof; Seeker wanted to reproduce observations; Shaper feared the proposed containment would annihilate the seventh race; Warrior would not abandon protections owed to innocents; Ascendant distrusted its accuser. Guardian eventually accepted Keeper's evidence and urgency. They faced a question with no clean answer: if containing the threat required destroying the structures sustaining millions, who could authorize that sacrifice?

The final containment would come to be remembered as **The Binding**. By then, the first Anchor had opened a breach in Aethyr's Veil. The seventh race's homeland lay at the heart of the devastation, and the struggle among the gods over responsibility, knowledge and intervention had ruined much of the ancient world. The First Anchor's activation triggered the initial catastrophe of the Second Sundering; the Divine War followed and deepened the devastation. The exact dates, military alignments and detailed sequence of wartime events remain open, while the order of these major events is established.

Keeper understood that the damaged region could not simply be abandoned. The original Anchor and the influence reaching through it had to be isolated from the rest of the Physical Realm. An immense containment, later called **the Shroud**, might preserve what remained beyond the epicentre. But it could not be sustained by an ordinary spell or a single contribution of divine power. It required the essences of **all Seven** as its continuing foundation.

Keeper knew the price with certainty.

The others could be persuaded to surrender a limited portion of their power to establish the containment. They might accept exhaustion, weakness or a long recovery for the sake of creation. What they would not knowingly be agreeing to was the surrender of their freedom: once the ritual took hold, **their essences would remain bound to sustaining the Shroud**, unable simply to withdraw and resume their former lives. Keeper understood this before asking them to participate and **chose not to tell them**.

The Seven came together for a final act of cooperation. Their trust was wounded, but they still recognized the danger to everything they had made. They joined their power believing the contribution would end when the Shroud stood. The boundary rose around the devastated homeland. The breach was contained. The spread of Nhal's influence was checked.

Then they tried to withdraw.

They could not.

The structure that held the Shroud was not merely a thing the Seven had built. Their own continuing essences had become its living foundation. They remained conscious and distinct, but bound to a task that would outlast their freedom. Keeper could no longer hide the truth: this had been the intended outcome all along.

**The Binding saved creation through a sacrifice the other gods had not knowingly chosen.**

For Warrior, the deception violated the honour of a freely given promise. For Judge, it violated the conditions under which agreement had been obtained. Seeker would search the structure for a means of release; Shaper would confront the terrible fact that the makers themselves had become the mechanism of their creation. Guardian could recognize the need to protect mortal lives and still be appalled that their right to choose had been taken. Ascendant, whose own earlier concealment had broken Keeper's trust, now faced a betrayal committed on a far greater scale. These particular reactions are dramatic interpretations, but Keeper's certainty and deliberate silence are established.

The Binding did not extinguish every road into the Shrouded Lands. Before the catastrophe, the seventh race's homeland had been a centre of the ancient Passage network, its gates linking scholars, travellers and supplies to distant civilizations. When the Seven raised the Shroud, the power sustaining those connections was drastically diminished. Great thoroughfares fell silent, some destinations were lost, and the immense movements of the Age of Wonders became impossible. **But a few surviving gates continued to function, quietly and at very low capacity, throughout the centuries that followed.** 

A handful of travellers could still pass where an army could not. A sealed packet, an artifact, a small party of researchers or a hidden agent could cross where a great caravan would once have moved. The Shroud had suppressed the ancient network without wholly erasing every route through its outer reaches.

Those limitations shaped the secret history of The Unmade. Some of its earliest collaborators had perished in the Anchor catastrophe; others remained outside the Shroud or learned which weakened gates could still be traversed. Across generations they moved small quantities of records and relics, placed operatives within distant institutions and kept scattered cells in contact through mortal messengers. They could not send an army to seize Covenant or Dominion lands, and they could not use the gates to recover their lost direct communication with Nhal. Their strength therefore lay in patience, local recruitment, infiltration and manipulation, not open military conquest.

In the modern awakening, rediscovered knowledge and revived magical infrastructure would make the gates newly important. Covenant and Dominion could eventually restore larger passages independently, bringing organized expeditions into the Shrouded Lands and contesting the ancient ruins. Yet the two alliances' earlier overseas incursions would continue to rely on ships, landing garrisons and supply lines across the sea. The hidden Unmade routes, the conventional continental war and the eventual Shrouded Lands PvP gateways were connected by history, but they were never the same system.

The Shroud did not purify the land it enclosed. The Seven had not destroyed Nhal's influence at its source; they had contained it. Beyond the boundary, surviving civilizations could rebuild without being continuously exposed to the First Anchor's corruption. Within it, the ancient homeland remained wounded. The breach endured, the ruined works of the seventh race lay under an unrelenting pressure of unmaking, and the Shroud prevented that pressure from spreading freely across Theia.

This made the Shrouded Lands both the grave of a great civilization and the enduring epicentre of The Unmade. Their first willing researchers had followed an intelligence they barely understood. Later cells preserved hidden sanctuaries, dangerous records and artifacts near the First Anchor while others travelled outward through the surviving low-capacity gates. They could send agents and small consignments, but not armies. Their presence in the wider world depended upon concealment and recruitment, not the conquest of either continent.

Not every figure moving among those ruins served The Unmade. Many transformed descendants of members of the seventh race were victims of the breach rather than conspirators. Some continued to defend halls, operate ancient devices or reclaim strongholds according to remnants of older duties. The Unmade might exploit such behaviour, but the corrupted inhabitants were not one obedient army. Nor did the relentless influence within the Shroud mean Nhal could speak clearly to every follower. The pressure of corruption endured through the Age of Silence even while the organization lacked reliable direct instruction from the intelligence it worshipped.

Yet the catastrophe had not extinguished every member of the seventh race. Beyond the greatest ruined cities, scattered groups preserved small sanctuaries and defended them against the pressure of corruption. They did not form one hidden kingdom. **Multiple small enclaves** endured, each guarding its own walls, memories and surviving knowledge, sometimes separated from the others by stretches of land controlled by The Unmade. Their survival required ordinary generations of work, sacrifice, repair and defence; they were neither immortal nor immune to the dangers surrounding them.

Not all of the seventh race's surviving scholars had been trapped behind the Shroud. Some had worked abroad before the Anchor's activation, studying with the great orders or visiting distant cities; others had fled during the catastrophe and the wars that followed, before The Binding closed around their homeland. Their numbers beyond the ruined region were small, and their connection to the disaster made an openly preserved Seventh identity dangerous in an age of suspicion, displaced populations and destroyed archives.

They possessed one gift no other mortal people shared. **Where the six original species could not ordinarily have children across species boundaries, the seventh race could form families with any of them.** Ascendant's extraordinary design had given them a compatibility beyond the limits of earlier creations. It was not an immunity to mortality or a promise that their children would wield immense magic. It was the ability for a family to cross boundaries that the other races could not cross biologically.

Over generations, the survivors outside the Shroud married into other communities, raised children among them and adopted the languages, loyalties and customs of their new homes. Descendants came to know themselves as human, dwarf, orc, elf, halfling or troll according to the families and societies in which they lived. War erased records; migration scattered relatives; names and the visible signs of their ancestry faded. A trace of the seventh race could persist in a family line long after every living member had forgotten the origin. A rare descendant might show an unusual magical affinity, but most would be ordinary people, not hidden superhuman heirs.

Within the Shroud, meanwhile, the small surviving enclaves maintained their distinct identity precisely because they remained isolated among the ruins of their homeland. The world beyond would remember an extinct people, even as their descendants lived unknowingly among the six races and their uncorrupted kin continued to fight for survival beneath the Shroud. The Guardians' potential historical links to those scattered scholars can be explored without requiring every Guardian to descend from them.

The outside world would come to speak of the seventh race as vanished, unaware that descendants still resisted within the Shroud. Much later, the independent Covenant and Dominion expeditions might encounter different surviving outposts near their restored gateways, finding possible footholds for the struggle over the ancient lands. The precise terms of those encounters remain to be written.

In the first generations beneath the Shroud, survival demanded more than courage. The old homeland had depended on elaborate magical infrastructure and distant suppliers. After The Binding, the survivors could no longer assume that a Passage gate would deliver food, that an ancestral ward could be repaired by its original makers, or that the road to the next city still existed. A sanctuary needed water, crops, storage, tools and people willing to maintain defences they barely understood. Members of the seventh race were capable of extraordinary scholarship, but scholarship could not substitute for every missing craftsperson or every lost harvest.

A proposed family history illustrates what this meant. An elder who had once worked among the great archives might spend the rest of a mortal lifetime teaching children to repair one protective pattern, not because the work was glorious but because without it a settlement would be exposed. Their children would know the pattern's purpose better than the theory behind it. A later generation might mistake a necessary safety condition for sacred ritual. Another enclave might preserve a different fragment of the same original design and accuse its neighbours of dangerous ignorance. The surviving communities were not one united kingdom waiting in suspended time. They were **separate enclaves**, forced by geography and corruption to make different compromises.

Some outposts could be built around surviving gate chambers, others around springs, fortified libraries or ancient machines that still supplied shelter. A few would abandon exposed positions and retreat deeper into protected structures. Others might fight to recover a storehouse or ward-tower because losing it would condemn an entire generation to hunger. Even contact with another uncorrupted enclave could be dangerous: a messenger might carry news, a needed replacement component, or unknowingly bring back corruption. The seventh race had not become immortal, invulnerable or uniformly powerful. They had become a people for whom ordinary survival demanded an extraordinary degree of vigilance.

Beyond their walls, the corrupted remnants of their civilization followed many different impulses. Some were capable of purposeful aggression under The Unmade's direction. Others seemed to repeat duties from before the catastrophe: a broken city guard protecting a gate whose city no longer existed, a damaged construct maintaining a ward that now harmed those approaching it, a former scholar attempting the same experiment again and again. These beings might attack anyone occupying an ancient stronghold, including The Unmade's rivals and later Covenant or Dominion soldiers. **Not every corrupted defender belonged to The Unmade's conscious hierarchy.** That distinction would eventually matter to adventurers asked to distinguish a deliberate cult operation from a tragic remnant of the old world.

The Unmade, meanwhile, possessed an enduring geographical advantage. Their heartland lay close to the First Anchor, where Nhal's corrupting pressure never ceased. They could occupy laboratories, recover relics and prey upon exposed communities, but the Shroud still prevented their master from freely incarnating and the weakened gates could not sustain a conquering army. Their organization had to preserve fragments of ancient expertise while also recruiting and indoctrinating new generations. Some leaders claimed to interpret signs from the corruption; others could only repeat instructions copied long before The Binding. **Unrelenting influence was not the same as unbroken, clear communication with Nhal.** That silence would help explain both their centuries of doctrinal disputes and the terrifying significance of later renewed contact.

The Guardians' relationship with the enclaves became equally complicated. A Guardian cell outside the Shroud might have knowledge of a surviving sanctuary and deliberately conceal it from an ambitious king. That secrecy could save the settlement from an expedition seeking forbidden knowledge. Yet the same cell might refuse to risk a rare gate route to deliver supplies, because exposing the passage could endanger several other enclaves. Inside the Shroud, survivors could reasonably ask why an organization founded partly by their ancestors had become more concerned with keeping the rest of Theia safe than with saving its own founders' descendants. There was no simple answer. The Guardians' duty to the world and the Seventh's duty to their families were not identical, even when both opposed The Unmade.

As the outside civilizations forgot the seventh race, the enclaves preserved different memories of their creator. Some might remember Ascendant as the god whose extraordinary gift made their civilization possible; others might regard the same ambition as the beginning of their ruin. Keeper could be remembered as the god whose Binding prevented the corruption from spreading, or as the one who enclosed innocent survivors in a land that could never be cleansed. **These specific traditions remain proposed cultural possibilities**, not confirmed religions or enclave names. They offer a way for future Seventh NPCs to disagree meaningfully without forcing all survivors into one historical interpretation.

In the modern era, the rediscovery of larger gateways could bring outsiders to the edges of these enclaves. A Covenant expedition and a Dominion expedition might separately encounter different Seventh outposts, each expecting to find only ruins and enemies. The Seventh could bargain for protection, provisions and the recovery of ancient infrastructure, while remaining wary of importing another mortal war into their homeland. Such outposts are a strong **working design for the first faction-separated PvP entrance bases**, but their exact agreements, sites and political leaders remain open. The alliances would not become friends merely because both met descendants of the same people.

The larger contested territory would then be a continuation of the old struggle, not a disconnected battleground. When a player guild occupies an ancient stronghold, it inherits walls, wards and supply routes that once belonged to the seventh race's civilization. Neglect, overextension or magical instability may permit corrupted defenders to reclaim it. In other cases, territory may become unclaimed or be disrupted by a magical world event, preserving the established range of neutralization outcomes. Seventh enclaves might also benefit from the liberation of particular sites, without becoming a third playable faction or an automatic ally of every guild. **A modern siege could therefore be fought over the same gatehouse that a Seventh family had defended for generations**, with the military contest concealing a much older and more personal history.

The collapse was uneven. That inequality explains why modern races retain different amounts of ancient knowledge. A city sustained by far-reaching passageways could perish when those connections vanished. A defensible metropolis supplied by stone roads, deep wells and local craft could survive while its outskirts crumbled. Libraries and workshops disappeared; masters died; surviving wielders suppressed some specific advanced dangerous techniques, while others fled, hid their teachings or taught new royal institutions. Some reconstructed knowledge incorrectly. **Exactly one original great magical order survived secretly**, though its identity has not been selected. Modern academies range from direct lineages to fragments and recent inventions. Their competition over recovered knowledge became intense long after the ancient cooperation was forgotten.

The Guardians entered the Age of Silence already divided by The Binding. Their founders had established cells and hidden repositories on both sides of the future Shroud during the Divine War, and the surviving gates could no longer support mass rescue. Those inside the containment helped protect independent Seventh enclaves while investigating the ruins; those outside pursued the broader duty of preventing another breach. The two missions were compatible in principle but often painful in practice. A desperate enclave could ask for supplies or defenders that an outside cell could not deliver without exposing the very route that kept other sanctuaries alive.

The Guardians' first great secret was the survival of The Seventh. Their earliest members **deliberately concealed the enclaves' locations**, fearing persecution by frightened kingdoms, exploitation by ambitious scholars and attacks by The Unmade. They concealed genealogies too, although Seventh refugees beyond the Shroud had already begun forming families with the other six races, a possibility unique to their ancestry. The founders believed secrecy would protect lives until the world could safely learn the truth.

But centuries transformed protection into ignorance. Couriers died; records were moved, sealed or lost; isolated cells handed down instructions without their original reasons. Some custodians retained fragments of the old history, while **many later Guardians did not even know the surviving Seventh enclaves still existed**. Others knew and feared that revelation would be another betrayal. A society founded to preserve evidence became capable of withholding it from its own members. The contradiction echoed Keeper's own conviction that protection could justify denying others the knowledge required to choose freely.

The Guardians never became a third kingdom. Their members served covertly among the races who would later form Covenant and Dominion, under separate native identities and with no public cooperation between the warring alliances. Their oldest records did not necessarily reveal Keeper's knowing deception in The Binding. Their secret purpose endured, but their understanding of the ancient catastrophe was never complete.

The Unmade's origin was earlier: certain scholars of the vanished people's era had deliberately followed the intelligence behind their discoveries. Their inner circles served Nhal knowingly; other adherents sought privilege, power or forbidden knowledge without grasping the ultimate purpose. The Second Sundering broke the ancient workings through which direct communication had been possible. For a very long period **the Unmade had no reliable contact with Nhal**. Its cells preserved instructions, distorted rituals and artifacts, but received no continuing stream of orders. Some cells disappeared; others embedded descendants in courts, academies, guilds, armies and religious institutions, waiting for the conditions that might restore contact.

The Guardians likewise survived by deep concealment, and their separate cells could disagree about how much truth to reveal. Exactly one of the three or four original great magical orders secretly survived. Its identity, original mission, means of survival, archives and modern relationship with the Guardians were left unanswered in Q436–444. **It must not be equated with the Guardians or treated as a confirmed separate institution in the absence of that decision.** The narrative must not quietly assign those choices to a named Order of Preservation or give it omniscient control of the world.

The **single surviving original magical order** deserves a place in the lived chronology without assigning answers the user never gave. In this **working institutional narrative**, some members of one of the three or four great orders recognized during the war that neither victory nor institutional continuity could be guaranteed. They distributed some records, destroyed or sealed techniques capable of reproducing the dangerous boundary work, and arranged for a narrow chain of instruction to survive under altered identities. Other orders were destroyed, exiled or fragmented, and their former members helped found public royal academies, guild colleges and religious schools. Those newer institutions were not merely inferior copies: they also developed techniques appropriate to a world with less accessible ambient power and fewer living masters.

The survivor's later generations could not preserve every ability of the Age of Wonders. Even authentic texts might describe effects modern wielders lacked the reserve or training to reproduce. Some teachings were useful in everyday construction or protection, some required lost infrastructure, and some were too dangerous to test. Its hidden influence might therefore appear through a teacher who knows an unusual proof, an anonymous correction to an academy's faulty reconstruction, or a prohibition recorded in a guild's oldest safety procedures. Its influence need not be a world-spanning secret government.

There is an unresolved political question at the centre of this institution. The Guardians' duty is to stop Nhal's return; a surviving magical order might see its duty as preserving knowledge even when the Guardians would suppress it. Alternatively they might cooperate selectively or not know one another exists. **Q436–444 did not receive user answers**, so the survivor's actual philosophy, whether it uses a sanctuary or unbroken succession, the extent of its archives, its relationship with Guardians and which cities hold its fragments are not established. The narrative above is an editorial model of how a survivor *could* matter over centuries, not a claim that those details were selected.

The earliest **Unmade** circles suffered a catastrophe of their own. Their ability to communicate with Nhal depended on ancient research and deep magical workings that the Second Sundering destroyed, disabled or sealed. **Their contact with Nhal ceased.** No continuing guidance could be assumed. A surviving agent might have a written instruction, a remembered phrase, or a pattern whose original context was lost, but there were no regular new commands from the Void. This change is essential to the order's history: for generations and then centuries, the descendants of Nhal's collaborators became custodians of silent promises.

Some believed Nhal had abandoned them. Others believed the silence was evidence that the sealing worked and must eventually be undone. Some preserved authentic diagrams, some miscopied them, and some transformed fragments of research into dogma. Entire cells died trying to recreate contact prematurely. Others learned patience, embedding themselves in restored academies, temples, courts and commercial systems, hoping to gain access to surviving artifacts when the conditions changed. The Unmade thus survived without being a perfectly coordinated omnipotent conspiracy. Its inner leadership preserved knowing devotion to Nhal, while outer adherents sometimes saw themselves as elite scholars, patriots or reformers.

Outside those secret lineages lay the devastated world. Refugees crossed functioning ancient passages while they lasted, trekked across territories whose old borders had vanished, and built temporary settlements beside rivers and defensible ridges. The divine catastrophe had not destroyed all places equally. People who knew how to feed themselves without ancient infrastructure survived differently from those whose economies had been built around it.

Human communities re-established themselves across the **great inland plains**, where farmland, rivers, roads and administrative institutions allowed the gradual formation of a unified kingdom. Their old settlements did not all disappear; some were repaired, others abandoned, and new towns developed around safer water and transport. Their human racial capital became a postwar inland centre of monarchy, not the later alliance capital. Other human centres served river trade, frontier defence and magical scholarship.

**Dwarves** possessed an immense advantage and a serious burden: their ancestral mountain/subterranean metropolis **survived the Divine War**. It held genuine old structures, but not complete instructions for their operation. Some holds retained their works; others were cut off when deep routes failed, forcing migration through newly developed surface roads. Some halls were sealed for safety and others emptied. The dwarf confederation retained local hold sovereignty even as shared military and engineering obligations grew. Volcanic forging settlements, a coastal harbour stronghold and a monumental stone surface city represented different postwar adaptations, not replacements for the ancient surviving capital.

**Orcs** rebuilt and expanded from their ancestral **grasslands**, preserving Great Clan identities, sacred lands, professional armies, champions and political councils. Families from displaced clans settled under the protection of relatives or allied Great Clans, while disputes over routes, grazing, farming and military obligations became part of later politics. Their orcish racial capital was a postwar grassland centre; sacred, military and coastal cities exercised different influences.

**Halflings** retained their inheritance from **underground valleys**. Some ancestral settlements and complex subterranean systems endured, some became dangerous and were sealed, and others were evacuated as old routes or water systems failed. Their migration toward orcish lands was shaped by a serious crisis whose exact cause remains open. The Great Clans offered sanctuary rather than making the halflings subordinates. Halfling guilds contributed arcane engineering, supply organization, transport mechanisms and defensive expertise. A modern surface racial capital and regional guild, manufacturing and internal-Dominion trade centres arose without obliterating their underground ancestry.

This cooperation formed the original core of **Dominion**. Orc military systems and halfling guild institutions grew into a sovereignty-respecting alliance whose High Ruler would be selected through trials involving champions nominated by four member civilizations. The enormous **Dominion capital** rose in the **highlands**, deliberately separate from the orcish grassland racial capital. Leadership was earned and recognized; no single Great Clan became permanent monarch over the other races.

**Humans and dwarves**, meanwhile, cooperated because food, metal, engineering and defence connected their recovery. Surface human agricultural routes supported mountain cities while dwarven fortifications protected human settlements. A royal pact became the basis of **Covenant**, protecting the sovereignty of dwarven holds and the human kingdom. The Covenant High Ruler was drawn from a **separate branch of the human royal dynasty**, while the human monarch ruled the inland-plains kingdom. The huge mixed-race **Covenant capital** emerged on the **coast** at a junction of allied routes, distinct from the human capital. The formation of Covenant and Dominion followed the Divine War by long mortal histories; they were not two direct continuation armies of the gods.

Two further divisions made the alliances emotionally impossible to untangle.

The original Guardian-created **elven civilization** did not immediately become two species. A dispute over magical **responsibility** grew: some argued the lost age demonstrated the danger of unchecked research; others believed the suppression of knowledge had helped make ancient catastrophe unavoidable. As the disagreement became religious and political, the **original elven capital was destroyed**. Exactly how is still undecided; an experimental disaster and civil war were explored but never selected as a definitive cause. Those who became **Aelari** established a woodland monarchy in primeval forest, worshipped Guardian and preserved Life, wards and ancestral spellcraft. Those who became **Vaelari** established an arcane-led mountain-lake capital, worshipped Seeker and prized research, magical perception and advanced combinations. Aelari aligned with Covenant, Vaelari with Dominion, carrying incompatible memories of the lost city and accusations into the war. Their modern rulers are **Queen Seralyth** and **First Archon Selrion**. An earlier suggestion that they had been close friends is only workable if historically before strict separation, and is not confirmed.

The elven separation also transformed the personal politics of later generations. Queen Seralyth and First Archon Selrion are confirmed leaders and names, but their exact ages and early biography remain to be decided. A proposed version places their first scholarly encounters in the era before the elven split hardened into permanent political separation: Seralyth admired Selrion's ability to test inherited doctrine, while Selrion respected her concern for the living communities affected by experiments. As public research became politicized, each came to interpret the other's caution or confidence as a failure of responsibility. The destruction of the original elven capital ended any possibility of sustaining that earlier trust. In the modern era their disagreement is not a secret friendly alliance. Each leads an enemy civilization, and their private memories, if retained, only make the inherited conflict more painful.

This history affects politics beyond their courts. An Aelari frontier settlement may refuse to surrender an ancient ward to Vaelari researchers because the loss of the original capital remains a warning against experimentation. A Vaelari academy may treat the same ward as evidence that Aelari secrecy keeps vital defensive knowledge from being understood. Rival patrols contest ruins and research sites; each faction's soldiers believe they are defending their people's future. The same artifact can thus have scholarly, religious and military significance without requiring a modern friendship across the alliance line.

The original **troll civilization** likewise had one ancestry and two co-creator traditions. The **Korrath** retained the ancestral **surviving highland capital**, councils of elders, sacred Keeper worship, old wards and chronicles. A population that revered Ascendant and emphasized growth and adaptation was **exiled**; it became the **Vorrath**, establishing a sacred **storm-coast capital** under religious leadership. The exact precipitating event is unresolved; competing claims of dangerous rituals, religious repression and falsified chronicles remain narrative possibilities. First Elder **Dromak** is a confirmed Korrath leader name; **Vashara** is a proposed Vorrath High Oracle. Korrath joined Covenant and Vorrath Dominion, carrying the exile's injustice and fear into different sides of a permanent war.

The Korrath–Vorrath division likewise became a living dispute over historical custody. In a proposed generational account, Korrath elders believed certain Keeper-linked protections were the inheritance of all trolls but must be controlled by those capable of maintaining them. Ascendant-revering communities argued that the same restrictions had become instruments of religious exclusion. Exile transformed a theological disagreement into dispossession: temples, highland routes, chronicles and burial sites remained behind, while families had to rebuild their lives along a storm-battered coast. Their descendants could remember the injustice more vividly than the disputed original rite.

First Elder Dromak, the confirmed Korrath leader, inherits both responsibility for an ancient surviving capital and the danger of preserving only those chronicles his council can bear to disclose. The proposed Vorrath High Oracle Vashara derives authority from sacred memory and the promise that exile will not be forgotten. Their relationship need not involve direct modern correspondence: rival religious proclamations, captured records, contested sites and the reports of soldiers can carry accusations across an impassable political divide. If a document later shows that the exile was more complicated than either tradition claims, Dromak and Vashara will face different crises of legitimacy. Their exact historical acts and Vashara's final name are not yet user-approved.

These histories were not detached from material life. Religious conflict meant disputed ancestral libraries, territory, pilgrimage roads and old monuments. The loss of a river crossing could starve a region. A surviving dwarf mechanism could be a national treasure, a dangerous unknown, or a strategic weapon. A halfling guild's recovered transport technique could sustain an army. Old elven manuscript claims could lead to raids on a ruin. Korrath and Vorrath chronicles could contradict the account used to justify exile. Each culture's relationship with the Divine War was alive in local government, supply routes, worship and family story.

Generations of conflict turned those grievances into an enduring **Covenant–Dominion war**. Each alliance possessed legitimate fears and remembered genuine harms; neither was wholly righteous or intrinsically evil. There was no normal friendly cross-alliance citizenship, trade, diplomacy or military service. Yet war required contact of another kind. Naval expeditions crossed seas. Military landing garrisons occupied enemy coasts. Scouts and patrols traveled roads used by local civilians. Fortified mines, salvage stations and research camps competed for scarce resources and artifacts. A Covenant fort on Dominion's continent remained an enemy occupation; a Dominion resource outpost in Covenant's woodland or mountains remained a hostile foothold, not a shared city.

Old trade patterns helped make those incursions possible. A road formerly used to move grain or ore became a military supply corridor; an old harbour that once served artisans became a naval landing; a forgotten valley pass held ruins valuable to archaeologists and generals alike. This did not restore normal trade between alliances: it explains how geography and infrastructure were repurposed by war. Smugglers, bandits, military requisition officers and profiteers existed within faction worlds and around borders, but any clandestine dealings with the opponent were exceptional and dangerous. Artifacts mattered not only because they were powerful. Some offered knowledge of material transformation, defences, healing, long-range Passage, or stabilization no modern nation possessed. Rulers funded expeditions in hopes of military advantage; honest scholars wanted to understand them; the Guardians feared uncontrolled boundary contact; the Unmade searched for components required to restore Nhal's connection.

To preserve player progression and meaningful danger, overseas outposts should be distributed across **level-appropriate frontier areas** rather than placed only in endgame zones. Early reconnaissance leads to raids on caravans, resource control, excavations, stronger fortress assaults and eventually major strategic installations. Players undertaking quests might collide with opponents protecting the same supplies. Enemy guards and patrols provide danger even without enemy players. The working example **Broken Coast** on Dominion land includes a Covenant beachhead, Dominion garrison and contested ore operations; **Ashen Reach** on Covenant land contains a Dominion landing, Covenant hill fort and quarry. The exact names and boundaries are not final. The **Shrouded Lands** remain inaccessible through ordinary travel until ancient Passage routes are unlocked for later larger-scale contested PvP.

The Guardians and Unmade exploited this same geography for very different reasons. A Guardian cell might send a native Covenant operative to recover a dangerous item from a Covenant expedition overseas, while a Dominion Guardian agent separately tracked an Unmade infiltrator in Dominion institutions. The two operatives served a single clandestine protective purpose without public alliance cooperation. An Unmade agent inside a garrison might secure an artifact by persuading a loyal officer it was strategically vital. The garrison would remain publicly loyal to its proper alliance. Thus the same outposts could host military missions, archaeological quests and deep secret-war stories across many regions and character levels.

The attempted peace was difficult because each alliance's internal politics carried different burdens. Covenant's hereditary High Ruler had to preserve the confidence of a human monarchy, independent dwarven holds, Aelari woodland rulers and Korrath elders. Dominion's trial-elected High Ruler answered to Great Clans, halfling guilds, Vaelari arcane institutions and Vorrath religious authorities. The negotiations were therefore a rare political exception, not a return to ordinary cross-alliance trade or diplomacy.

The confirmed modern leaders **Queen Seralyth** of Aelari, **First Elder Dromak** of Korrath and **First Archon Selrion** of Vaelari carried inherited schisms into their governments. The additional proposed cast—Covenant High Ruler Alaric Veyne, human Queen Elowen Veyne, dwarf High Thane Bromund Stonevein, Dominion High Ruler Draven Korr, halfling Guildmaster Tessa Brindle and Vorrath High Oracle Vashara—provides useful personal motivations but is not yet user-approved by name. Alaric may fear sacrificing alliance unity for peace; Elowen may fear abandoning frontier dead; Bromund may resent wars draining defensive engineering; Dromak may protect chronicles whose disclosure could inflame religion. Draven's authority rests on trials and earned leadership, Tessa on guild livelihoods, Selrion on research, Vashara on exile and sacred memory. No ruler is automatically an Unmade agent merely because their policies are exploitable.

The Unmade chose the negotiations as a target precisely because genuine peace would reduce military secrecy, lower demand for desperate weapons and allow rulers to investigate anomalies that had been dismissed as enemy sabotage. Its agents operated through native institutions on both sides. The attack destroyed the delegation, planted conflicting evidence and made both populations believe their ancient enemy had betrayed a last opportunity for peace. This was the **Shattered Delegation**, whose Unmade authorship is established, while exact victims, weapon and venue remain proposed.

The Guardians' proposed response was fractured and painful. A cell may have recognized suspicious artifact transfers but lacked sufficient information to stop the attack. Afterwards Guardian operatives protected witnesses, searched records and debated whether revealing incomplete evidence would trigger purges or compromise their agents. Their covert channels could cross political lines, but public Covenant and Dominion forces never cooperated. The failure deepened the Guardians' internal argument over secrecy, even as war expenditure and military expeditions created more cover for the Unmade.

The political road toward the Shattered Delegation did not begin with a sudden love of peace. A generation of overseas raids made every alliance capital aware that the sea was no longer a safe separation. Fortified resource hubs consumed taxes and guild supplies; repeated attacks interrupted agriculture and shipping; families living near enemy landings bore the cost of a war their rulers described in grand religious language. Some commanders argued for greater invasions to end the threat permanently. Others calculated that even a successful campaign would leave the opposite continent impossible to occupy fully. Merchant and craft institutions **within each alliance** complained about military requisitions, not because they enjoyed ordinary friendly trade with the enemy, which remained forbidden.

In a working personal history, the Covenant High Ruler Alaric Veyne inherited the duty to hold four sovereign member civilizations together while the human Queen Elowen Veyne defended the autonomy of her own monarchy. They could agree on the danger posed by Dominion yet disagree on whether an exceptional negotiation was prudence or betrayal of the frontier dead. Dwarven High Thane Bromund Stonevein saw irreplaceable engineers and supplies diverted from protecting old holds; Seralyth worried that a negotiated archaeological boundary might expose ancient elven sites; Dromak feared that religious concessions could invite demands for chronicles his elders had sealed.

Across the sea, the proposed Dominion High Ruler Draven Korr had earned authority through trials and could lose legitimacy if Great Clans regarded compromise as weakness. Halfling Guildmaster Tessa Brindle saw the cost of sustaining long overseas supply chains and the opportunity to redirect engineering to civilian works. Selrion regarded access to ancient research as a strategic question, not merely a theological one. The proposed Vorrath High Oracle Vashara feared any agreement that treated ancestral exile and sacred claims as permanently settled. None of these characters must secretly serve Nhal for the political conflict to be real. The Unmade's advantage was that it could exploit each of these sincerely held concerns.

For the peace attempt to happen at all, supporters in each alliance had to overcome genuine objections. That explains why its destruction was so effective. The Unmade did not need to manufacture hatred; it needed to make an exceptional and fragile attempt at reconciliation appear to confirm every inherited warning about the enemy.

At one exceptional moment, exhausted Covenant and Dominion rulers contemplated **peace negotiations**. This was not normal diplomacy but a rare effort to interrupt generations of conflict. The negotiations promised to reduce casualties, redirect scarce resources and permit some societies to look beyond immediate military survival.

The Unmade feared that peace would destroy the conditions on which its survival plan relied. War kept rulers suspicious, justified dangerous research and concealed missing shipments, altered records and illegal archaeological work. Its secret agents **orchestrated the Shattered Delegation attack** and framed each alliance for the destruction. The exact weapon, attack leader, attendees and location remain working matters, but **The Unmade's authorship is confirmed**. Both Covenant and Dominion emerged certain the other had betrayed the attempt at peace. Losses were carried into families and governments. A possible group of mortal hardliners may have been recruited or manipulated, but the event was directed by the servants of Nhal, not by an independent cabal alone. The war hardened.

The Guardians suspected a concealed hand but lacked proof they could reveal without compromising their network. Some Guardians feared that public disclosure would simply cause purges or inspire rulers to exploit the forbidden magic uncovered. Others felt that concealment was repeating Keeper's failure. Their choice to remain hidden protected their members while enabling the Unmade's narrative to spread.

The attack's aftermath also altered the incursion system that players would later encounter. Coastal commanders on both sides demanded stronger arrival forts, patrols and early-warning stations. Engineers fortified resource hubs because losing a mine or quarry could cripple military construction. Researchers accompanied soldiers because newly uncovered ancient structures promised a strategic advantage, and the Shattered Delegation had demonstrated that a hidden magical device could alter politics as decisively as an army. Each newly established enemy outpost generated local grievances: farms were requisitioned, roads became dangerous, and rival soldiers treated the same supply convoy as either necessary protection or an occupying force's lifeline.

The Guardians followed these changes with alarm. A military research camp could legitimately investigate a lost ward, but it could also be penetrated by an Unmade operative. A Guardian agent assigned to investigate an artifact could not reveal the order's existence to an entire garrison, nor could they ask an enemy alliance for public assistance. Covert contacts therefore embedded investigations inside ordinary reconnaissance, resource escorts, sabotage and salvage quests. This is why the later level-banded overseas incursion design is not merely an extra PvP activity: it is the physical geography of the hidden war, with parallel narrative tracks on both continents and no violation of permanent player allegiance.

Then, after countless generations of silence, **Nhal began to be heard again**.

The same changing magical conditions awakening rare mortal potential also reactivated fragments of old infrastructure. A long-inert mechanism responded to a new wielder. An ancient device whose purpose no scholar remembered began producing subtle distortions. Some Unmade families possessed manuals warning that particular phenomena would precede restored contact. At first signals were sporadic: a suggestion, an improbable correct insight, a pattern recurring across independent experiments. The inner leadership did not immediately know whether this was Nhal or a trick perpetrated by rival Unmade cells. They tested it through increasingly dangerous magical workings.

Once responses became reproducible, the meaning was terrifying. The generations who had preserved silent rituals were no longer alone. Nhal's selective communication resumed—not with every cell and not constantly, but through certain rare resonances strengthened by the world's awakening. Inner Unmade leaders received direction after millennia of extrapolating from old fragments. Their preparations became coordinated in a way that had not been possible since the Second Sundering. Old caches and buried plans acquired meaning; agents within royal courts, guilds, militaries and academies were instructed to acquire components, obstruct investigators and accelerate research into ancient links.

The distinction between the ancient First Anchor and its modern imitations would one day become a matter of survival for adventurers. The original device had not been left beneath the Shroud because its material was impossible to break. Its activation had made it part of the persistent breach in Aethyr's Veil. Once the damaged boundary and the structure were entangled, destroying the visible mechanism could tear away the remaining framework holding the rupture in check. The Seven had been forced to contain the region through The Binding because destruction might have made the breach worse.

The Unmade who survived the catastrophe inherited no complete blueprint for that achievement. Their ancestral knowledge was scattered among damaged records, forbidden fragments and artifacts whose purpose later generations only partly understood. Through the long centuries without direct communication from Nhal, they experimented with inferior reconstructions. As ambient magic awakened and Nhal's whispers began returning, those experiments became more effective. But the devices they built were **not second First Anchors**. They were lesser resonators that exploited the old damage and drew limited influence outward through secondary connections.

This difference made resistance possible. A small resonator hidden in a mine or temple could be smashed after its defenders were defeated. A larger regional Anchor might require its supporting wards and conduits to be disconnected before the device could be safely destroyed. The most sophisticated modern Anchors, assembled from rare artifacts and recovered research, might demand a prolonged expedition or raid to stabilize and sever their connections. The established progression distinguishes **Minor Resonators**, **Regional Anchors**, and **Major Anchors**; exact numbers and placement remain open. None was as complete as the ancient original, and destroying one did not itself heal the original breach. The Guardians learned to distinguish these constructions from the First Anchor and to warn allies against simply striking an unfamiliar magical device without understanding what sustained it.

The Unmade's purpose was cumulative. An isolated lesser Anchor could disturb a village or draw corrupted creatures to an ancient ruin; a regional installation could extend that influence across a frontier; **a coordinated network of major Anchors** might finally strain the Shroud and the divine essences bound to it. The two warring alliances would encounter different pieces of the same secret network through local investigations, contested excavations and overseas military incursions. Each destroyed modern Anchor would be a genuine victory, but the greater threat would endure beneath the Shroud until the original breach itself could be confronted.

**This is why The Unmade is an increasingly dangerous modern threat rather than an omnipotent ancient conspiracy that could have conquered the world at any time.** For most of the mortal age its supreme source of direction was silent. Now it is beginning to speak again. Its servants are more prepared than the Guardians realize, while Nhal is still constrained and not yet able to act freely.

The awakening itself is not evil. Major cities can benefit from more healers, better wards, revived engineering and more gifted citizens. Rural populations may gain abilities previously unavailable. Mythic creatures may return, some dangerous, others simply long absent. But instability near deep ancient structures can also expose vulnerable individuals to contact. Academies disagree over restriction versus experimentation. Militaries fund research for strategic advantage. Traditional engineers and magical scholars compete over which methods are safe and effective. This landscape is precisely what Nhal wants: rival institutions discovering powerful truths without sharing the warnings that would let them interpret those truths correctly.

A player enters Theia amid these consequences, not amid exposition about the two primordial powers. As a human, dwarf, Aelari or Korrath they belong permanently to **Covenant**; as an orc, halfling, Vaelari or Vorrath they belong permanently to **Dominion**. The opening story depends on their culture. A royal frontier garrison betrays a village's trust. Ancient dwarf mechanisms wake beneath surviving halls. Aelari sanctuary wards fail. Korrath wardstones cease to respond. Great Clans dispute a champion's honour. Halfling guild engineers expose sealed ancestral works. Vaelari research accidents threaten neighbours. Vorrath religious signs divide pilgrims. These are meaningful local dramas independent of Nhal.

As players explore, the alliance war brings them to enemy continental outposts and contested resources. The same military expedition may put them near a stolen relic that Guardian observers have been tracking for generations. An Unmade cell may appear at first to be an academic fraud, an ambitious supply officer, a religious zealot or a corrupt commander. The Guardian who first aids the player is not introduced as the member of a new playable political faction; that identity is revealed cautiously, because open affiliation could make the player's alliance accuse them of treason.

As an adventurer advances, the same names and places acquire new meanings. A low-level player might escort a supply wagon because the road is dangerous. Later they learn that one crate contained components for an ancient stabilizing device. At a higher level, a Guardian observer may ask them to trace the device's origin through an enemy-continent expedition. Eventually they discover that a legitimate scholar who signed the shipment order was manipulated by an Unmade intermediary. This structure lets local military, economic and scholarly quests remain independently satisfying while quietly forming a continuous historical investigation.

An orc player's clan challenge and a human player's frontier corruption story need not mirror one another, but both can introduce questions about loyalty and manipulated evidence. A dwarven sealed hall and a halfling ancestral mechanism can preserve different technical fragments of the same vanished Passage system without requiring those races to exchange teachers in the modern era. Aelari wards and Vaelari experimental methods can lead to competing interpretations of a shared ancient catastrophe; Korrath and Vorrath records can frame the same exile as defence or oppression. These parallel perspectives should remain separated by faction and then converge only in the creator's omniscient account, not in a cross-faction party or public peace treaty.

The launch story unfolds across roughly six **non-mandatory thematic level bands** toward the working cap of sixty: racial beginnings; regional exploration and early dungeons; military and overseas incursions; ancient magical awakening; exposure of Unmade infiltration; and the defeat of a dangerous Unmade attempt to create a persistent Void anchor. Each alliance has separate protagonists, quests, staging grounds and final operations. There is no mixed-alliance party or public reconciliation. Covenant and Dominion can independently disrupt parts of a common Unmade plan while remaining each other's enemy.

The level-sixty victory is consequential. The player helps defend their own alliance against an ancient working and defeats a senior Unmade operation. The Shattered Delegation's secret authorship may finally be revealed to selected characters, though not necessarily accepted by governments as public truth. An authentic ancient document hints that the Great Betrayal of the Divine War may have been intended to prevent something worse. The final raid concept, **The Severed Chain**, concerns severing the anchor's supports, not killing Nhal as an ordinary creature.

Beyond this first campaign lie the Shrouded Lands and the rediscovery of ancient Passage. The two alliances will enter separately and contest ruined territory. Lost documents may expose the manipulated accusation against Ascendant, the tragic truth of the vanished seventh race and the incomplete memory of the Seven. The surviving great magical order, Guardians and Unmade will compete over what is recovered. One potential endgame dilemma is whether to restore the catastrophic old seal, risk removing it, or compel the Seven to reunify into Aethyr—possibly destroying their independent selves. Another working possibility involves mortals maintaining a new boundary through complementary, independently operated mechanisms. **None of these endings is final canon.**

The world that Aethyr created through self-sacrifice has not yet understood the cost of that act. Its seven successors built lives and civilizations while their own virtues turned against one another. Nhal could not defeat Aethyr directly; it nearly triumphed by breaking the trust of what Aethyr left behind. The Unmade repeated the pattern with Covenant and Dominion, first preserving a devotion through centuries of silence, then manipulating fresh mortal grievances when their master finally began to whisper again.

That is the historical line linking creation, divine relationships, the seventh race, the Second Sundering, ancestral migrations, surviving cities, royal treaties, clan refuge, racial schisms, the Shattered Delegation, offshore occupations, ancient artifact hunts, the Guardians, The Unmade, and the opening experiences of players. None is an isolated lore event. Each is an inherited consequence of a world that has repeatedly tried to save itself without knowing whether the cost was shared justly.
