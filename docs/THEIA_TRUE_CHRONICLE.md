# BOOK ONE — THE WORLD BEFORE THE WORLD

## Prologue: The Choice That Made Existence

There was no first day.

A day requires a sun, a turning world, the passage of measurable time. Before such things were possible, there were only two primordial wills: Aethyr and Nhal.

Aethyr was not a benevolent king of light. It was the principle that something might become. In its nature lay emergence, distinction, change and the inexhaustible potential of what did not yet exist. Nhal was not a demon born of shadow. It was the contrary principle: negation, cessation, the annihilation of possibility. Neither initially possessed a physical body; no world yet existed in which a body could stand.

Their opposition grew into an immeasurable contest. Aethyr could not transform Nhal into becoming. Nhal could not extinguish the potential represented by Aethyr. Every attempted victory reasserted the incompatibility between them. Ultimately Aethyr understood that continued struggle was unwinnable. Any final direct defeat would leave Nhal supreme.

Its last possibility was a choice so terrible that it would destroy the one being making it.

Aethyr broke itself.

That was the **First Sundering**. Its released substance became the foundation of physical existence: space in which separations could persist, time in which events could unfold, matter capable of form, and magic as an underlying property of reality. As the new universe unfolded, a new condition emerged: what could exist was distinguishable from what was beyond existence.

The convulsion cast Nhal outside the created realm into the **Void**, a condition of absolute physical nothingness. There was no dark palace there, no plain upon which Nhal might walk, no ordinary time against which it could measure its exile. Its primordial nature alone endured.

Aethyr did not endure as one mind. Seven conscious fragments awoke from the shattering, each wholly individual and intrinsically related to the others.

They would be remembered as the Seven.

**The universe was born not from effortless victory but from a sacrifice made because victory had become impossible.** In ages yet to come, the descendants of that sacrifice would make another desperate choice, and the world would call it betrayal.

## I. The Seven Without a King

The Seven found one another gradually. At first they could perceive no reason for the familiarity that drew them together. Each possessed fragments of power and instinct, but none had an intact recollection of Nhal or the primordial act that gave them life. None could claim to be the original consciousness reborn.

The Guardian discovered that what existed could be preserved and healed, but that preservation demanded effort, and sometimes sacrifice. The Seeker learned to discern principles beneath appearances and took delight in mysteries. The Warrior found that strength was inseparable from action, endurance and will. The Shaper understood how to give existing potential form and build what had never been built. The Judge recognized laws linking causes to consequences, promises to obligations, and power to limits. The Keeper found the boundaries that allowed powerful workings to remain stable. The Ascendant discerned how powers might be combined, amplified and adapted beyond familiar capability.

These were specialties, not monopolies. Each could grow beyond an initial instinct, and magic was not theirs to invent. They learned its laws as later mortals would, albeit on an immensely different scale. Rare creative acts demanded combined divine power.

There was no supreme member of the Seven. They were equals, and the first shared principles arose through discovery and agreement, not royal decrees.

Friendship formed. The Guardian and Warrior became closest, alike in resolve to protect but divided over the price a promise should command. Where the Warrior feared abandoning honour in moments of desperation, the Guardian feared allowing innocent lives to perish for a rigid rule. The Seeker and Shaper became intellectual companions; a theory in one mind became a marvel in the other's hands. The Judge mediated disputes without claiming to rule. The Keeper withdrew into questions whose dangers were not easily explained; the Guardian showed affection, the Seeker irritation at secrecy, and the Judge conditional respect.

The Ascendant stood increasingly apart. Brilliant in the arts of synthesis and improvement, the Ascendant watched the Shaper receive the greatest mortal admiration and began to mistake acknowledgement for justice. Shaper's dismissiveness was often careless rather than cruel. It wounded nevertheless. The Seeker suspected hidden experiments, the Keeper feared unauthorized changes, the Judge defended shared limits, the Guardian saw philosophical conflict, and the Warrior kept distance. Ascendant had no close ally among the Seven.

It is important that their failures developed slowly. Their gifts made the world. Their flaws made them vulnerable to a force that understood what those gifts meant to them.

## II. The Peoples of the Gods

The Seven created living peoples through fundamental magic and divine essence. No single race was a copy of one god's entire character, and being created by a god did not compel permanent worship or political obedience.

The Guardian shaped the elves, attuned to living systems and the responsibility to safeguard them. The Warrior created orcs capable of extraordinary endurance, courage, martial skill and complex communal honour. The Shaper created dwarves whose talents for construction, material knowledge and enduring works came to distinguish entire mountain civilizations. The Judge created humans, adaptable and inclined toward complex institutions and laws. The Keeper created halflings with an ancestral connection to magical resilience and the capacity to maintain stability through change. The Keeper and Ascendant together created trolls.

The trolls' making was the first enduring rupture in the Seven's trust. The Keeper had planned extraordinary resilience stabilized by dependable magical structures. The Ascendant contributed ambitious synthesis and adaptation techniques, but also introduced an unauthorized alteration. The Keeper discovered this after too much had been committed to reverse without putting the new people in jeopardy. Rather than punish the trolls for the actions of their makers, the Keeper completed stabilizing work. Trolls flourished as a people in their own right. They were not cursed, inherently corrupted or destined to choose between good and evil.

The breach was real. The Ascendant saw innovation unappreciated; the Keeper saw confidence violated. The others drew judgments consistent with their principles, and the old trust did not wholly recover.

Meanwhile mortals grew beyond the purposes imagined for them. Human cultures developed their own laws; orcish Great Clan traditions, dwarven holds, elven social and magical traditions, halfling guild foundations and troll highland institutions took individual forms. Their civilizations spread through forests, mountains, grasslands, plains, underground valleys and highlands. Worship changed with experience. Humans created by the Judge eventually looked chiefly to Guardian. Halflings created by Keeper came to revere Seeker. The elves and trolls would later divide into different patron traditions.

Divine essence continued through descendants, most often subtly and sometimes through rare empowered bloodlines. The fall or transformation of a creator would alter a relationship to that inheritance, not erase the people or make them magically impossible.

For a long time these developments delighted the gods. Mortal individuality seemed to show that creation had succeeded. Yet it also forced a question they had never settled: did a creator retain the right to constrain what had been created?

# BOOK TWO — THE AGE OF WONDERS

## III. The Mortal Discovery of Magic

Magic permeated the world before mortal civilizations learned to study it. Every casting drew on an internal personal reserve, directed by intention and practiced technique. Natural affinity and bloodlines mattered, but learning and concentration mattered too. Training expanded capacity and improved efficiency. Reserves regenerated naturally and could be restored through ambient influence and disciplined meditation. A depleted reserve normally prevented casting; overambitious complex workings could produce mental overload and loss of control.

Over centuries, scholars distinguished **six disciplines**, their names still provisional in present-day lore: **Elements & Energy**, controlling elemental and energetic phenomena; **Matter**, shaping physical substances; **Life**, working upon living and spiritual processes; **Form**, establishing patterns, conditions, wards and magical stability; **Mind**, governing perceptions, illusions and limited emotional effects; and **Passage**, directing spatial transfer and connections.

These were six perspectives on a common magical reality, not rival religions. Simple workings might use one. Advanced work combined several, but an individual had to master each discipline involved in a working. Gathering untrained casters together did not bypass the requirement. True permanent matter created from nothing, stable self-sustaining enchantments, extraordinary restoration of the dead and long-distance passages belonged largely to dangerous or forgotten ancient mastery.

That structure explains why the Age of Wonders accomplished things the present age struggles to reproduce. The ancients did not merely possess more raw power. They had greater knowledge of how to shape and maintain complex interactions between magic, physical infrastructure, geography and life.

## IV. The Great Orders

Three or four great independent magical orders arose, distinguished by intersecting philosophies, specialist work and civic responsibilities. Numerous smaller schools and hereditary traditions existed beside them.

The orders shared libraries, exchanged scholars, held councils and traveled through sophisticated Passage networks connecting widely separated civilizations. They drew on several gods' teachings, occasionally received direct divine instruction, and sometimes heard from trusted mortal intermediaries. Importantly, they could openly contradict the Seven. A mortal scholar who proved a divine model incomplete might earn the Seeker's fascination, the Judge's attention or the Shaper's admiration.

The great orders were generally cooperative until the Divine War. Disputes over experimentation, ownership of discoveries, obligations to rulers and the ethics of living creation were serious, but still contained within a culture of debate. This matters: the first age was not an idyll without conflict, but disagreement had not yet become an excuse to destroy one's opponents.

A working intellectual map describes orders concerned with understanding, making, preservation and exploration. Their final number, institutional names and precisely which survived are not fixed. **Exactly one original great order remains secretly alive in the modern age**, but its identity and surviving knowledge are unresolved. Some current academies may descend directly from ancient teaching lines; others possess only displaced fragments or modern reconstructions.

The Shaper became exceptionally beloved among ancient mortals because of tangible improvements in construction and living environments. Seeker–Shaper collaboration accelerated discovery. Keeper's caution sometimes seemed obstructive, while Ascendant's improvements could appear either inspiring or recklessly competitive depending upon the witness.

In such an age it was almost inevitable that somebody would seek the boundary of mortal possibility.

## V. The Seventh People

The Seven and their mortal scholars contemplated a new people: one whose relationship with fundamental magic would be exceptionally broad, precise and adaptable. Their creation required more than routine power. The Shaper and Seeker, in the leading working account, drove the conceptual breakthrough; the Ascendant supplied synthesis and amplification; other gods contributed protective, lawful and stabilizing principles.

The project was controversial even before disaster. The Judge asked what rights the new people possessed and who would answer for unintended harm. The Guardian insisted they must not be treated as expendable experiments. The Keeper worried that the sophistication required would introduce hidden instabilities. The Warrior questioned whether the Seven could protect a people they scarcely understood. The Seeker wanted full investigation; the Shaper saw an extraordinary possibility; the Ascendant saw an opportunity at last to contribute something no one could ignore.

The seventh people were created. Their name is not finally chosen; an earlier provisional name resembling Aethyr was abandoned for clarity.

They were neither tools nor monsters. They grew into an independent civilization with genuine beliefs, ambitions and moral variety. Their extraordinary relationship with the six disciplines allowed discoveries that astonished older scholars. In the best of their work, advanced patterns could sustain spatial connections, living systems and enormous civic structures with precision unimaginable to later ages.

But at the extreme boundary of this research lay a danger that none of the Seven had yet recognized. Some workings reached beyond arrangements *inside* creation and disturbed the conditions by which creation remained separate from the Void.

The first response from beyond was almost indistinguishable from insight.

A problem that defeated brilliant scholars suddenly seemed solvable. A researcher tried the elegant arrangement that had appeared in a flash of inspiration. It worked. Others reproduced parts of the result. Fame followed; schools copied the method; ambitious minds asked what else might be possible.

Nhal had noticed.

It could not stride out of the Void. But through the disturbance it could perceive, then suggest, then encourage further experiments that strengthened the fragile contact. Some researchers remained entirely unaware of a source outside their own thinking. A few recognized an external intelligence and chose to seek it knowingly. These became the first foundations of the hidden order later called **The Unmade**.

The seventh people were not inherently corrupted. Their achievements supplied an opening; Nhal supplied the deception.

## VI. The Hidden Work of Nhal

Nhal remembered a truth the Seven did not: they were fragments of the primordial power that had chosen dismemberment rather than allow Nhal to prevail. Their greatest strength lay in combinations that echoed Aethyr's lost unity. No individual god possessed the whole, and the Seven did not yet know why.

Nhal therefore pursued division, not immediate conquest.

To the Seeker, it made certain discoveries irresistible. To the Shaper, it magnified the perceived duty to protect an extraordinary civilization. To the Judge, it helped real violations appear as proof of a wider deliberate crime. To the Guardian, it exposed risks so horrifying that delay seemed callous. To the Keeper, it made secrecy seem safer than discussion. To the Warrior, honour demanded transparent decisions at precisely the moment the others became incapable of openness. To the Ascendant, old rejection became renewed proof of conspiracy.

The influence was never complete possession. Nhal was limited by access through exceptional workings and by the choices of individuals. It could reveal genuine fragments of knowledge, omit context and exploit existing mistrust. It had help from willingly committed mortal collaborators who could alter documents, sabotage research and mislead authorities in ways Nhal itself could not physically accomplish.

The resulting catastrophe would belong to Nhal's design, but not only to Nhal's responsibility.

# BOOK THREE — THE SCHISM AND THE DIVINE WAR

## VII. The First Accusation

An inquiry into the seventh people's most extreme experiments uncovered a disturbing pattern. Some techniques relied on modifications developed through Ascendant's synthesis research. Other evidence suggested concealment. That much could be true. But the decisive accusation — that Ascendant had deliberately endangered or controlled the new people for personal power — was supported by testimony and records manipulated through Nhal's mortal intermediaries.

Keeper remembered the secret changes during troll creation. Judge demanded that shared law be respected. Seeker insisted that unexplained phenomena could not yet serve as proof of guilt. Shaper feared that closing down research would kill or impoverish an innocent civilization. Guardian feared for that civilization's safety. Warrior believed any judgment required honest evidence and a real chance of defence. Ascendant, certain of longstanding hostility, concealed earlier actions and rejected restriction.

What began as an investigation turned into a dispute over who could be trusted to investigate.

Keeper withheld dangerous knowledge rather than risk wider replication. Seeker pursued inquiry despite constraints. Shaper sheltered some researchers. Judge moved to enforce collective limits that no longer enjoyed collective consent. Guardian prepared protective measures outside the council. Warrior answered threats and perceived dishonour. Ascendant saw each restriction as a weapon.

None was wholly without reason. Each used the others' conduct as proof that compromise had already failed.

## VIII. The Fracturing of Mortals

The war did not begin with two cleanly defined divine armies. Several conflicts overlapped: defensive interventions, retaliations, religious disputes, attacks upon laboratories, disputes over the survival of the seventh people and military alliances formed for local mortal interests.

The great magical orders tried to remain connected longer than many of the gods. They continued to share warnings and sought to preserve archives as towns and kingdoms polarized around competing divine teachings. Eventually transportation networks were militarized, sanctuaries were attacked, and rival political authorities demanded exclusive obedience.

Ancient mageworks that had supported ordinary life became strategic targets. A captured passage could move armies across enormous distances. A destroyed ward could deprive a settlement of generations of accumulated protection. Attacks on civic networks sent consequences cascading outward into communities that had never taken a side.

Early military victories concealed broader failure: the world supporting civilization was being dismantled.

A dwindling number of scholars attempted to reconstruct what had truly changed at the boundary of existence. Their findings were incomplete. Some were suppressed to prevent experimentation; other archives were deliberately altered to protect reputations or conceal forbidden acts. Countless practitioners died without teaching successors. People would spend millennia mistaking torn research notes for complete magical doctrine.

## IX. Keeper's Discovery

In the leading working narrative, Keeper eventually identified a pattern connecting apparently unrelated magical disasters. The failures were not simply the result of excessive power. Certain structures formed recurring disturbances at the boundary with something beyond physical existence.

Keeper concluded there was a conscious external pressure — Nhal, though that primordial identity remained unknown to the gods — drawing ever nearer through their ruined research networks.

The danger was too great to ignore, but Keeper had spent ages teaching others to distrust secret knowledge, and now possessed truths difficult to demonstrate without reproducing the threat. Judge demanded evidence available to all. Seeker wanted independent testing. Shaper feared that shutting down the network would devastate the seventh people. Warrior wanted assurances for innocent communities. Ascendant thought Keeper was reviving an old accusation to settle a score. Guardian, carrying a persistent burden to protect life, finally accepted the urgency.

The same relationships that once made creation possible now made action by common agreement all but impossible.

## X. The Great Betrayal — A Second Desperate Choice

**This is the most developed proposed account, not a final user-approved answer to the ancient betrayal.**

Keeper and Guardian devised a vast sealing arrangement that would reinforce the boundary between creation and Void. Its components were not isolated stones in a forgotten cave. They were connections embedded in the ancient world's greatest magical infrastructure: city systems, stabilizing structures, passage networks and installations tied to the seventh people.

The plan demanded appropriating systems trusted to the care of many civilizations and gods. No consent sufficient for such a transformation could be obtained. Some argued more time might yield another solution; Keeper believed there was none. Guardian saw the potential annihilation of everything they loved.

They acted.

The betrayal was real because allies had entrusted these systems to them for another purpose. Even if the motive was salvation, the appropriation stripped others of choice and placed innocent lives at terrible risk.

As the sealing began, Nhal exploited its instability. The vast working succeeded in reducing contact with the Void but turned against part of the world that sustained it. Connections failed in cascades. Ancient passages collapsed. Great cities were annihilated. Magical systems that had supported entire regions fell silent.

The central lands were shattered. The vanished seventh people disappeared from known history. The surviving gods were scattered, transformed, diminished or lost under circumstances still open to final design.

Thus the Second Sundering preserved the world from an immediate existential danger while destroying the world many mortals had actually known.

It did not prove the Guardian and Keeper had acted rightly. It proved only that their intervention had consequences as immense as their desperation.

The Divine War did not truly end because one army surrendered. It ended because the old civilization could no longer sustain its wars.

## XI. What Was Left

The gods withdrew, fell silent, vanished or were changed; exact destinies remain open. Divine essence remained in their mortal creations, though such links could transform.

The majority of the original magical order network was destroyed. A few practitioners survived as scattered teachers, royal servants, exile scholars, secret preservers and founders of later institutions. Exactly one original great magical order endured clandestinely. Its final identity is unwritten, and it remains distinct from the order of Guardians founded during the Divine War.

Those later called the **Guardians** arose among people who realized that neither the divine accusation nor the final catastrophe explained everything. They preserved selected warnings about the external threat, forbidden techniques and evidence of a hidden influence. Secrecy was their protection against panic, political exploitation and dangerous replication of experiments; it was also their ongoing moral flaw.

The earliest Unmade collaborators had helped create and deepen the schism. The Second Sundering blunted their direct access to Nhal, but some survived with records and procedures they did not fully understand. They endured as scattered circles and eventually consolidated into a secret organization devoted to undoing the boundary and allowing Nhal to enter.

An ancient war ended. Its two hidden consequences outlived it.

# BOOK FOUR — THE SCATTERING AND THE MORTAL KINGDOMS

## XII. A World Without the Old Roads

For mortal peoples the years following the Second Sundering brought migration, starvation, loss of infrastructure and political fragmentation. Cities that had depended on ancient networks lost power, trade and water systems. Refugees traveled through the few surviving passages while those routes still functioned, sometimes arriving in regions far from their ancestral homelands.

Human populations expanded and rebuilt across great inland plains. Dwarves kept their remarkable ancestral subterranean metropolis alive; their confederated holds preserved building and defensive knowledge through extraordinary difficulty. Orcish peoples consolidated Great Clan traditions across grasslands. Halflings protected surviving guild craft and memories of underground-valley ancestry. Elves and trolls survived while their internal religious debates hardened.

One ancient Korrath highland capital endured, giving later keepers of chronicles unusual continuity. Yet survival was not the same as historical accuracy. Even genuine records could be read incorrectly, changed deliberately, or locked away.

Over thousands of years the past receded. Communities remembered their own losses first. Priests taught divine interpretations consistent with their patron. Scholars argued from contradictory scraps. Families inherited stories that no longer distinguished what had been witnessed from what had been believed.

Different peoples developed competing versions of the Divine War, sometimes claiming that the gods they worshipped had stood on a single righteous side. In truth the divine conflicts were never so simple.

## XIII. The Human Crown and the Dwarven Holds

The human kingdom emerged across the agricultural plains, its inland capital a source of law, grain and military power. The dwarven holds occupied defensive mountain environments, producing unmatched engineering, stonework and equipment but relying on some surface supplies and transit.

Cooperation became necessary. Human agricultural networks and exposed settlements benefited from dwarven construction and defence. Dwarven mountain gates benefited from access to plains trade routes and allied military strength. A royal treaty eventually established the first durable political partnership that grew into **Covenant**.

A vital feature of the agreement was **autonomy**. Dwarven holds did not become human provinces. The human kingdom did not become identical with the larger alliance. A separate branch of the human royal dynasty inherited the office of Covenant High Ruler, while the principal monarch continued ruling the human kingdom. The enormous mixed-race **coastal Covenant capital** developed separately from the human **inland racial capital**.

Later Aelari and Korrath political accessions occurred through security obligations and negotiated membership, not absorption into human citizenship. Covenant came to embody mutual duty, continuity and the principle that power must be held to enduring obligations, though its rulers could also use those ideals to defend secrecy or unpopular wars.

A working foundation myth has been called the "Treaty of Crown and Stone"; any exact royal hero, founding war and treaty name still requires approval.

## XIV. Orcish Great Clans and Halfling Refuge

The orcs' grassland civilisation was neither barbarous nor politically simplistic. Great Clans maintained territorial obligations, sacred places, military schools, professional armies, renowned champions and traditions of honour. Their confederation did not eliminate clan identity.

Halfling society, originally rooted in underground valleys, developed guild governance and highly sophisticated mechanisms and magical engineering. A profound displacement — the specific ancient crisis remains open — brought refugee halfling populations into orcish territory.

The Great Clans offered refuge and protection. The gesture did not strip halflings of their government or craftsmen of their independence. Halfling engineering, logistics and guild organization strengthened the partner civilization; orcish defences and territory provided security.

That relationship eventually matured into **Dominion**. It was not a conquering empire directed by one race. Each member government retained sovereignty. Dominion's High Ruler was selected through **trials**, with each of the four later member civilizations able to nominate champions. Authority could be achieved through martial excellence, but also through judgement, negotiation, service and the capacity to lead.

The alliance's immense mixed-race **highland capital** served all four peoples. Its civic identity differed deliberately from Covenant's hereditary coastal seat.

Dominion's ideals of sovereignty, earned authority, adaptation and ambition were never synonymous with Nhal's ideology; the alliance is not secretly a dark faction.

## XV. The Two Elven Peoples

The ancestors of Aelari and Vaelari were one Guardian-created elven people. Their later religious and magical divide centred on responsibility in the pursuit of knowledge.

Some argued that the Divine War proved the necessity of restraint, protective Life and Form techniques, and the protection of inherited sanctuaries. Others argued that ignorance, withheld truths and prohibitions had nearly destroyed the world and that rigorous experimentation offered the only defence against repeating the disaster.

Over time the dispute became political, then religious. The original elven capital was destroyed in the schism, though the precise destruction mechanism remains unwritten. Each side remembered the city through a history that made its own arguments more convincing.

The **Aelari** established a woodland monarchy in the primeval forests, chiefly revered Guardian, preserved ancestral techniques, and became members of Covenant. **Queen Seralyth**, their confirmed ruler name, inherits both cultural dignity and the burden of protecting a people who fear dangerous experimentation.

The **Vaelari** established an arcane-led civilisation around a mountain lake, chiefly revered Seeker, championed advanced research and magical perception, and became members of Dominion. **First Archon Selrion** is the confirmed name of its principal leader, who prizes inquiry and underestimates risks associated with intellectual confidence.

The two peoples are related but deeply hostile. Earlier speculative prose made Seralyth and Selrion former personal friends. Under the confirmed **strict no-cross-alliance** setting, that personal past is only tenable if it predates final separation; otherwise it must be replaced by an indirect historical rivalry. A modern open friendly relationship is not canon.

## XVI. The Two Troll Peoples

The trolls were created jointly by Keeper and Ascendant and originally shared a rugged highland civilisation. Their co-creators' old disagreement became a lasting interpretative fault line among mortals.

The ancestral tradition prized Keeper's stabilising role, the custody of old wards and the survival of sacred records. Another tradition honoured Ascendant's contribution, the right to improve and adapt, and resistance to inherited prohibition.

The conflict became sufficiently severe that a religious population was **exiled**. The **Korrath**, who retained the ancestral surviving highland capital, principally revered Keeper and governed through an elders' council. The **Vorrath**, who departed, chiefly revered Ascendant and established a sacred storm-coast capital under religious government.

Both tell histories of what happened. To Korrath, ancestral protections were endangered; to Vorrath, a legitimate faith was suppressed. Precisely what event triggered exile, whether sacred chronicles were altered and which participants committed the decisive wrongdoing are detailed *working* story questions, not settled facts.

**First Elder Dromak** leads Korrath; proposed **High Oracle Vashara** leads Vorrath. This rivalry eventually bound Korrath to Covenant and Vorrath to Dominion, intensifying a cultural rupture with centuries of renewed war.

Neither troll society is inherently good or evil, nor does its patronage lock its citizens out of any magical discipline.

## XVII. The Institutions of Eight Civilizations

As the modern ages advanced, all six magical disciplines remained learnable by every race. Their institutions, not biological prohibitions, produced recognizable differences.

Human royal armies, knightly orders and martial schools flourished alongside protective and structured magical traditions. Dwarven holds developed defensive warfare, superb equipment, siege engineering and Matter–Form–Energy skills. Orcish Great Clans preserved different professional martial and magical specialisms. Halflings mastered **all three** combat engineering, precision tactics and defensive techniques, expressing their arcane expertise in devices, logistics and wards.

Aelari retained Life arts, forest protection and ancestral wards. Vaelari promoted magical analysis, experiments and advanced multidiscipline research. Korrath preserved highland warding, records and established rites. Vorrath evolved religious and coastal applications shaped by storms, exile and Ascendant doctrine.

The mundane and magical arts competed for resources and prestige. They also depended upon each other. In major cities magical services became familiar while remote settlements often relied primarily on conventional crafts. Medicine took different cultural forms. Military mages served as supports, integrated specialists and in rare cases exceptionally powerful wielders. Ordinary soldiers countered magic with equipment, formation tactics and trained anti-mage teams. A legendary unenchanted weaponmaster could be as consequential as a mage in a different way.

Modern academies ranged from state-controlled to guild, religious and autonomous institutions. They fought for influence and recognition, while the true Age of Wonders remained a distant, fragmentary ideal.

# BOOK FIVE — THE WAR THAT NEVER ENDED

## XVIII. Covenant and Dominion

Each alliance established an independent homeland across one of Theia's two principal continents. A possible cartographic arrangement places Covenant west and Dominion east with the Shrouded Lands between; direction and exact map shapes remain provisional.

The Covenant comprises **humans, dwarves, Aelari elves and Korrath trolls**. Dominion comprises **orcs, halflings, Vaelari elves and Vorrath trolls**. These are permanent playable allegiances. The two states are sovereign, mutually hostile, and conduct no normal friendly trade, joint governance, exchange of citizenship or cooperative military activity.

Their conflicts could not be reduced to the original Divine War. The modern alliances formed long afterward. Present hostility came from territorial claims, strategic access, religious wrongs, the two elven and two troll schisms, contested historical relics, inherited reprisals and genuine political mistrust. Nhal's servants sought to exacerbate all this, but did not invent every reason for war.

A Covenant village harmed by an overseas raid needed no ancient demon to justify anger. A Dominion family bereaved by a Covenant assault needed no magical manipulation to resent its enemy. The world remained populated by mortals with legitimate loyalties and choices.

Working modern protagonists among its rulers include **Alaric Veyne**, Covenant hereditary High Ruler; **Queen Elowen Veyne** of the human monarchy; **High Thane Bromund Stonevein** of the dwarf holds; **Queen Seralyth** of the Aelari; and **First Elder Dromak** of the Korrath. Across the sea stand proposed **High Ruler Draven Korr** of Dominion, **Guildmaster Tessa Brindle** of the halflings, **First Archon Selrion** of the Vaelari, and **High Oracle Vashara** of the Vorrath. Only the names Seralyth, Dromak and Selrion were explicitly confirmed as personal names; the remaining names and detailed biographies are working proposals.

Alaric is conceived as a careful administrator vulnerable to concealment in the name of unity; Elowen as a forceful human sovereign impatient with caution; Bromund as a defensive engineering expert wary of outside magical interference; Seralyth as a protector whose responsibility can become obstruction; Dromak as a guardian of inconvenient historical evidence. Draven's pride in earned leadership can harden into rejecting compromise; Tessa's faith in engineering can obscure moral consequences; Selrion's intellectual confidence can become dangerous zeal; Vashara's longing for vindication may lead her to embrace an incomplete account of ancestral exile.

These are motivations to develop, not proof that any ruler is an Unmade servant.

## XIX. The Shattered Delegation

For generations the alliances fought and sometimes exhausted themselves into uneasy military pauses, but no durable peace was secured. At one exceptional moment, leaders authorized an attempt at negotiation.

This attempt was unlike normal life in either alliance. It was rare, guarded and dangerous. Delegates came under their own banners, accompanied by their own trusted protectors. Their people had reasons to mistrust the proceedings, but there was enough hope to alarm enemies of peace.

That enemy was **The Unmade**.

The Unmade had survived as a clandestine legacy of Nhal's first mortal adherents, rooted in ancient forbidden inquiry and renewed over centuries. Its highest members knew they served the primordial presence outside existence. Their methods were not always dramatic occult rituals. They used mundane political influence, altered orders, stolen research, military secrets and people who valued power or certainty.

Unmade agents infiltrated arrangements surrounding the delegations. The leading working account has them planting a concealed magical device, arranging its activation and fabricating evidence that would separately lead each faction to blame the other. The exact weapon, venue, number of dead, intended secondary stolen relic and operation commander remain under author development. **The authorship of the attack is not ambiguous: The Unmade directed it.**

The attack shattered any nascent trust. Delegates died, survivors brought mutually incompatible testimony home, commanders mobilized, and both alliances publicly blamed their ancient enemy. Leaders suffered personal losses. The old war accelerated.

To ordinary people the event would become proof that the opposing alliance could never be trusted. To The Unmade it was a successful intervention preventing two exhausted powers from turning their attention to strange new magical activity. War supplied justification for extraordinary research, destruction of records, military secrecy and desperate attempts at technological advantage.

The Unmade did not want Dominion to triumph or Covenant to triumph. It wanted both unable to recognize the greater danger.

## XX. The Guard Against the Void

The **Guardians** existed almost as long as the mortal memory of the Divine War. They were founded by individuals who discovered parts of the external manipulation and understood that the great enemy had not truly been destroyed. They preserved selected forbidden techniques and history, but no complete revelation of Aethyr and Nhal's primordial relationship.

Their agents held ordinary public identities in both alliances, recruited covertly and compartmentalized knowledge to protect surviving cells. Some watched ancient sites; others preserved archives, recovered artifacts or intervened against suspicious experiments. Their choice to remain secret helped them survive, but it also caused errors. A proposed Guardian warning before the Shattered Delegation went unheeded because its network moved too cautiously.

Guardian operatives did not form a public neutral government, allow opposing races to settle freely in each other's capitals, or grant players an alternate political allegiance. An agent who was publicly a Covenant citizen remained one; a native Dominion Guardian operative did the same. Sensitive information might travel invisibly within Guardian structures, but public action respected permanent faction separation.

The Guardians stood in opposition to The Unmade, which pursued Nhal's return through sustained anomalies and ancient anchors. Their war took place inside ordinary institutions, often invisible even to rulers.

A third presence also endured: the **one surviving original great magical order** of the Age of Wonders. Its historical mission and relationship with the Guardians are unresolved. It could preserve knowledge that either hidden order needs, but it must not automatically be merged with either.

# BOOK SIX — THE AWAKENING

## XXI. The World Begins to Change

The primordial danger had never vanished. The Second Sundering had restricted Nhal's access, not destroyed Nhal. Across thousands of years the ancient defensive conditions gradually changed. Whether this was entirely a sealing network's deterioration, a consequence of magical cycles or a combination with deliberate interference remains a working metaphysical question.

Ordinary people saw effects before they saw explanations. Individuals discovered unusual new ability. Exceptional wielders exceeded familiar limits. Ancient structures stirred; lost enchantments responded to nearby magic; strange creatures returned to wild places. Research into dangerous ancient techniques became newly fruitful. Some sites experienced hazardous instability, while others offered benefits that modern engineers and healers scarcely dared imagine.

The awakening itself was not evil. Theia's deep creative potential was becoming available again. Nhal's servants benefited because some extraordinarily sophisticated projects could reopen contacts with the Void.

Modern institutions reacted according to culture and philosophy. Aelari protected threatened woodland wards; Vaelari scholars argued for research; dwarf engineers investigated deep machines; halfling guilds debated recovering transport works; Great Clans weighed sacred honour and military duty; Korrath guarded ancient wards; Vorrath interpreted storms and pilgrimage signs; human courts disputed how much dangerous research should be regulated.

The Unmade anticipated opportunities, positioned agents near key institutions and used the continuing war to conceal its movement of artifacts and materials. The Guardians perceived the pattern only imperfectly, increasingly aware that a danger they had watched for generations was gaining momentum.

## XXII. A War Across Both Continents

Covenant and Dominion had once been described as almost wholly isolated continental powers, but the later confirmed design is more dangerous and more active: **each maintains military outposts on the opposing continent**.

These are not guest towns, shared settlements or evidence of peaceful integration. They are foreign fortified landings and occupied military facilities surrounded by hostile sovereign territory.

Covenant maintains reconnaissance posts, garrisons, resource depots and research detachments in Dominion lands. Dominion maintains Great Clan military camps, halfling engineered posts, forward strongholds, salvage sites and arcane expeditions in Covenant lands. Ships, military transport, guard patrols and defended routes keep them operational.

This makes war a lived reality during leveling. A player ventures overseas to scout, rescue captives, disrupt a convoy, recover intelligence, harvest scarce materials or investigate a strange ruin; opposing players are undertaking their own related missions. Native guards, patrols, ambushes and dangerous geography give risk even without player enemies.

A working Dominion coastal warzone called **Broken Coast**, around levels 24–30, includes a Covenant defended landing, Dominion inland garrison, ore basin, roads, ruins and research. A working Covenant coastal warzone called **Ashen Reach** offers the mirror strategic problem in different terrain: Dominion landing, Covenant hill defence, contested quarry and forest routes. Names, exact layouts and brackets are provisional.

Secure arrival and revival interiors, anti-camping guard behaviour, level-band protections, multiple routes and PvE-completable objectives are recommended design safeguards, not yet finalized mathematical rules. Temporary control of resource sites should matter strategically without permanently starving one faction of essential crafting materials.

The Shrouded Lands remain different: the catastrophic central ruins await ancient passageway restoration and ultimately become a broad contested territorial PvP region. Early overseas incursions make the conflict visceral before that later opening.

## XXIII. The Shadow Campaigns Across the Map

The Guardians and Unmade can use the same incursion geography as the visible military war, but for hidden purposes. This was explicitly raised and refined in voice design conversations.

A seemingly ordinary Covenant scouting operation might put a player near an Unmade excavation on the Dominion continent. A Dominion resource patrol might uncover stolen magical materials at a Covenant-held fortress on its own land. A Guardian contact can ask a player to follow a set of clues across several zones and level bands, sometimes back into an earlier region where new information changes its meaning.

These are **multiregion, multi-level story threads**, not a one-off dungeon quest. Players on opposite allegiances can unknowingly foil separate branches of one Unmade plan, but **never openly cooperate, share parties, change faction allegiance or serve the enemy's leadership**.

The same frontier mechanics can support military supply convoys, Guardian artifact recovery, Unmade sabotage, covert observation, timed assaults, and hazardous research containment. This makes investment in the custom engine's incursion system valuable both mechanically and narratively.

## XXIV. The Hero Is Not Foretold

Players enter Theia as ordinary members of their own civilization. Their adventures matter because they act, learn, defend neighbours, confront injustice and earn reputations — not because they are the sole predestined saviour.

Eight distinct opening environments express cultural histories:
- Human **Royal Marches**: frontier garrison corruption and failures of duty.
- Dwarven **Deepward Holds**: old structures stirring beneath inhabited halls.
- Aelari **Greenward**: sacred woodland protection failing.
- Korrath **First Highlands**: ancestral wardstones falling silent.
- Orcish **Great Grasslands**: disputed Great Clan challenge and oaths.
- Halfling **Guilded Valleys**: guild excavation uncovering sealed ancestral machinery.
- Vaelari **Lakeward Spires**: experiment, scholarly ambition and public safety.
- Vorrath **Stormward Coast**: pilgrimage, religious disagreement and an anomalous sacred site.

They are working region names, not locked map coordinates. The locations serve as culturally genuine places with ordinary conflicts as well as faint threads of deeper mystery.

Some regional quests investigate corrupt tax collectors, ore operations, lost travellers, unusual wildlife, shipping, guild ownership and family feuds. Not every antagonist is Unmade, and not every glowing ruin is a node of the Void.

The world must remain compelling even to adventurers who never follow the cosmic plot.

# BOOK SEVEN — THE FIRST RECKONING

## XXV. Levels One Through Sixty

The MMO's launch level cap is a **working 60**, with broad narrative movements rather than a mandatory campaign track.

In the first levels a character comes to know a homeland and its culture. Later routes introduce other allied civilizations, roads, wilderness, guilds, military obligations and early dungeons. Around the mid-level journey the character is drawn into frontier wars and overseas expeditions, the Shattered Delegation becomes significant, ancient infrastructure awakens, and unfamiliar patterns begin linking otherwise unrelated regional disasters.

Guardian operatives first appear as ordinary investigators, historians or soldiers, not heralds of a neutral faction. Players might initially confuse them with another conspiratorial society. In the later journey they learn of **The Unmade**, infiltrators whose crimes include the destruction of the rare peace negotiations.

The campaign should resolve local threats along the way. A dwarven forge can be saved without defeating Nhal. An orcish champion can be exposed without any supernatural control. A halfling infrastructure crisis can have genuine guild rivalry at its heart. These are stories of a living world, not filler preceding a mythological cutscene.

## XXVI. Covenant's Reckoning

Covenant's working central narrative has been described as **Oaths in Ashes**. Its royal and military institutions discover that trusted insiders falsified information, redirected supplies and suppressed records associated with the Shattered Delegation.

Human commanders, dwarf engineers, Aelari wardkeepers and Korrath chroniclers encounter apparently separate anomalies. In a proposed story, an undercover Guardian historian helps the player understand why different ancient devices display related magical patterns. First Elder Dromak may recognise an authentic chronicle that contradicts popular religious accounts, yet fear the consequences of full disclosure.

The player exposes a significant Covenant Unmade cell whose operatives have turned legitimate excavation, procurement and security authority toward an ancient mechanism. A senior conspirator escapes or reveals a shadowy superior, possibly the working antagonist called **the Architect**.

When an ancient structure threatens a major Covenant region, Covenant's armies and specialists independently mobilize. Players help defend settlements, disrupt hazardous devices and defeat the immediate Unmade operation. Their victory is real: the threatened region survives. Yet a recovered ancient testimony hints that what mortals call divine betrayal may have been an attempt to prevent an even worse calamity.

## XXVII. Dominion's Reckoning

Dominion's independent campaign has been tentatively titled **The Price of Dominion**.

Great Clan politics, halfling engineering, Vaelari magical analysis and Vorrath sacred tradition each supply clues to a hidden conspiracy. A military intelligence trail connecting the Shattered Delegation to internal manipulation collides with dangerous excavations and research. Selrion's scholars identify relations within ancient workings; guild engineers debate whether those relations indicate one distributed structure. Religious specialists find warnings whose significance they cannot confidently interpret.

A local Unmade officer or researcher is exposed within Dominion's own institutions. High Ruler Draven and other political figures face genuine decisions about defending their lands while preserving sovereignty and military credibility. No one needs to consult Covenant or request its help.

Dominion specialists and adventurers attack a distinct threatened site on their own continent. They disrupt the active mechanism and prevent its regional catastrophe. The resulting documents suggest that an unknown power beyond the Seven was entangled in the lost war.

Neither alliance knows precisely what the other has learned. Neither renounces hostility or becomes an ally of the enemy. In the creator's full history, both victories obstruct different parts of The Unmade's plan.

## XXVIII. The Severed Chain

A working launch-level raid concept is **The Severed Chain**.

A network of ancient mechanisms is reactivated through stolen knowledge and clandestine manipulation. Its purpose, unknown to most of the technicians powering it, is to sustain a dangerous contact at the Void boundary.

The raid is not a conventional battle against Nhal. Nhal remains unable to enter physical reality as an ordinary boss. The adversaries are Unmade officers, compromised ancient guardians, unstable mechanisms and possibly a limited manifestation maintained by an incomplete anchor.

Each alliance has a completely separate appropriate operation; there is no shared party, joint raid or mirrored diplomatic finale. Success defeats a credible threat without permanently erasing all Unmade cells or resolving Theia's cosmic mystery.

At level sixty, a player can reasonably know that an organized ancient conspiracy engineered the Shattered Delegation and that standard accounts of the Divine War are missing crucial facts. They do **not** need to know the name Aethyr, the complete origin of Nhal or the truth of every god's fate.

# BOOK EIGHT — THE YEARS NOT YET PLAYED

## XXIX. The Return to the Shrouded Lands

Beyond launch, forgotten Passage mechanisms begin to respond to recovered knowledge and stronger magic. Their reopening reveals routes to the devastated central lands previously inaccessible to routine ships and conventional overland travel.

Covenant and Dominion arrive through separate routes and establish competing war positions. Neither regards the other as a partner. They fight for resources, ruins, strategic heights, access to magical infrastructure and valuable knowledge. Their old grievances intensify because each believes it has the right to prevent the enemy from exploiting the scar of the Divine War.

The territory also provides ruins and documents capable of challenging both sides' inherited mythology. Ancient connections across the seas become apparent from matching architecture, geology and magical structures.

A broad persistent territorial PvP system can grow here from the overseas incursion mechanics already learned during leveling. The Shrouded Lands are not just another level-30 quest zone; their opening marks a major new era.

## XXX. The War Over History

Later creator-facing arcs could reveal the truth about the altered divine accusations, the extent of Nhal's early manipulation and the existence of the vanished people. Competing scholars from Covenant and Dominion may reach related conclusions *independently*, while the Guardians and Unmade fight to secure or destroy the records needed to prove them.

The surviving original magical order's true nature may emerge. Its understanding of how to build, preserve or diagnose ancient workings could supplement Guardian warnings without guaranteeing the two institutions agree.

Religious upheaval would be uneven. Aelari and Vaelari would react according to rival doctrines of restraint and discovery; Korrath and Vorrath would revisit the Keeper–Ascendant dispute and the historical exile. Human, dwarven, orc and halfling institutions would each reinterpret the discoveries through their own politics and inherited lore.

Even if the truth about the old gods becomes undeniable, it would not automatically settle the modern war, restore dead relatives or erase occupied lands.

## XXXI. The Lost Seventh People

The vanished people are one of the story's deepest moral obligations. If their extraordinary gifts created Nhal's first contact, they still were not thereby guilty of existing.

One working resolution places surviving descendants in an isolated refuge produced during the Second Sundering. Other fates remain possible. The final name and nature of their civilization remain to be written.

Their eventual reappearance, if they survived, would confront both modern mortals and whatever remains of the Seven with the unanswered question of creator responsibility. Why were these people made? Why were they kept alive at such risk? Who authorized the last sealing, and who paid for it? Were there other possible choices?

That discovery should matter more than merely providing a new enemy race to kill.

## XXXII. Nhal's Return and the Last Choice

Nhal's motive is primordial. Physical creation excluded the power of unmaking. It seeks a condition under which its consciousness can exist stably within reality, then remake the rules that prevent its dominance. It advances through willing agents, manipulated research, and exceptional anchors rather than omnipotent instant control.

The Second Sundering may have held Nhal back by creating a distributed burden across ancient magical infrastructure. Simply restoring the old seal could repeat its original sacrifice or suppress the beneficial awakening. Allowing it to fail could permit incarnation. Forcing the seven gods back into Aethyr might destroy beings who have become separate persons.

A proposed fourth direction is to recover dispersed knowledge and independently maintained structures across mortal civilizations, achieving a more stable boundary without a new catastrophic single act. This would not mean reuniting Covenant and Dominion as a political alliance or permitting ordinary multi-caster cooperative spells: separate specialists and installations could perform their own operations. **The ultimate solution and Nhal's actual fate remain open.**

The narrative theme that unites the proposed ending with the confirmed beginning is not that all differences must disappear. Aethyr sacrificed its unity to make independent existence possible. The Seven almost destroyed that legacy when they could no longer trust each other. Mortal peoples may finally learn to preserve a shared world without surrendering every difference that makes them themselves.

## The Six Disciplines and Mortal Limitation

Every one of the six playable races can eventually study all six disciplines; none is biologically locked into one. Racial cultural specialties, instructors, hereditary affinities and regional institutions influence the ways they develop.

**Elements & Energy** directs energetic and elemental effects. **Matter** transforms material. **Life** concerns living, biological and spiritual systems; extraordinary resurrection is severely constrained. **Form** stabilizes magical structures, conditions and wards. **Mind** works with illusion and perception; it is not cheap unlimited mind control. **Passage** manages spatial links; great long-distance gateways are largely ancient lost practice. Single users must master every discipline their working requires. Divine combined creation and independently maintained infrastructure do not contradict that mortal rule.

Magic uses limited internal reserves, recovered by time, meditation and ambient influence. Training increases capacity and efficiency; concentration and knowledge determine control. Complex workings can mentally overload a practitioner; ordinary exhaustion prevents further casting. Self-sustaining ancient enchantments and matter created permanently from nothing are exceptional lost crafts.

Martial heroes are viable without spells. Human knights and royal schools, dwarf engineered defences, orc professional champions, halfling combat engineers/precision/defenders, elven divergent techniques and troll highland/exile traditions remain culturally distinctive. Magic and technology compete in civilian life; established cities have magic more commonly than isolated villages.

## The Public War and the Hidden War

Covenant and Dominion remain permanently hostile and prohibit normal public cross-alliance cooperation. Their geographic borders are not absolute walls: each maintains fortified military outposts and reconnaissance sites on the enemy's continent. Those locations are hostile occupations in sovereign enemy territory, not integrated mixed-alliance settlements. Open-world PvP, enemy guards, resource clashes, protective staging garrisons and scalable incursion stories begin before the Shrouded Lands' later unlock.

The Guardians and Unmade recruit from both sides under deep cover. The player can develop a secret investigative relationship with Guardians without becoming a neutral third-faction citizen or teaming with enemy players. War incursions and undercover Guardian/Unmade missions can share the same reusable frontier systems across multiple level bands and both continents.

## Canon ledger — what this manuscript does not decide

The following remain unfinalized despite detailed narrative proposals: the Seven's individual personal names, appearances, final states or exact wartime sides; the final true mechanics, actors and timing of the Great Betrayal; the vanished people's proper name and surviving fate; names/archives of the one surviving great magical order; definitive quest/dialogue/dungeon mechanics; complete continent geography and political calendars; combat class/spell lists/numerical balance; details of PvP level scaling; ultimate cosmic conclusion.
